# 06. Choosing a host country

*Chapter scope: which cooperating state should host the chartered floating village, judged on political economy as well as engineering, and how to negotiate with the winner. Shared assumptions are in [00-design-basis.md](00-design-basis.md); the legal pathway is in [01 §7.3](01-legal.md); the engineering site scores are in [03 §1.10](03-site-and-structure.md). Governance indices are the World Bank's 2026 WGI release (2025 data, 0 to 100 scores) and Transparency International's CPI 2025. All URLs accessed November 2026.*

## Summary

- **Panama survives the test, narrowly, and not for the reasons the synthesis gave.** On a weighted matrix of nine political-economy criteria plus the chapter 03 engineering score (30% weight), Panama scores **72**, the UAE **71**, Malaysia (Johor, a candidate added in this chapter) **70**, Indonesia (Riau) **59** and the Maldives **57**. The top three are within the error of the method; the gap to the rest is not.
- **The Riau Islands lose their engineering lead on politics.** Riau is 5 points ahead of Panama on engineering and 21 points behind on political economy. Indonesia has no legal route to devolved civil law, its sea-space permit (KKPRL) cannot be transferred or mortgaged, its Constitutional Court has already struck down private coastal-water concessions, and the 2023 Rempang riots happened in the same free-trade zone. Indonesia only beats Panama if engineering carries more than about 80% of the weight.
- **Panama's real weakness is the instrument the study recommends.** Panama's Supreme Court annulled two legislatively ratified concession "contract-laws" in 26 months: First Quantum's copper mine (November 2023) and CK Hutchison's canal ports (January 2026). Both fell on procedure (direct award without tender, missing Comptroller countersignature, inadequate public interest and consultation). A bespoke Host State Agreement passed as a contract-law would walk into the same trap.
- **The key instrument in Panama is therefore a framework statute, not a contract-law:** a special marine economic area law modelled on Law 41 of 2004 (Panamá-Pacífico), creating an autonomous agency, a 75 to 99 year tenure that overrides the 20-year port-law cap, and a competitive award, with Comptroller countersignature, Escazú-standard consultation, Law 54 stability registration and treaty-protected structuring.
- **Timing matters.** Panama plans a constituent assembly in 2027 and a new constitution from 1 January 2029. The recommended sequence is a pilot module under ordinary law (vessel registration plus a 20-year seabed concession) while the framework law waits for the new constitutional settlement.
- **Verdict: Amber.** A credible host exists, but no candidate offers a durable, self-governing concession off the shelf.

## 1. Method

**What is scored.** Nine political-economy criteria (A to I, below) were scored 1 (poor) to 5 (good) from the evidence in Sections 3 to 9, then combined with chapter 03's engineering score. The scores are the author's judgement on cited evidence, not measurements. Weights reflect the study's own finding that "what kills seasteads is politics, insurance and money" ([08 synthesis](08-synthesis-and-verdict.md)): durability of the deal carries the most weight.

| Code | Criterion | Weight within political economy | What a 5 looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Legal hooks and tenure | 15% | Existing special-zone statute, sea-space tenure ≥ 75 years, floating structures recognised |
| B | Durability of the deal | 20% | Record of honouring ratified concessions through changes of government; no annulments |
| C | Governance quality | 10% | WGI rule of law and regulatory quality > 70; CPI > 60 |
| D | Political stability and security | 10% | WGI political stability > 75; no recent constitutional shocks or armed conflict |
| E | Treaty and arbitration cover | 10% | ICSID member; investment treaty with ISDS covering US or UK investors |
| F | Practicalities | 10% | Residence route for families, low tax, hard currency, banking, hospital, airport, labour |
| G | Social licence | 10% | No customary or indigenous marine tenure at likely sites; low fishing conflict |
| H | Geopolitics | 5% | Aligned with residents' home states; low sanctions and conflict exposure |
| I | Civil-liberties fit | 10% | Residents can worship, speak, assemble and govern their own affairs freely |

**Engineering.** Chapter 03 scores are used unchanged where they exist (Riau 85, Panama 80, North Malé 79, Tarawa 67, São Tomé 58). For candidates chapter 03 did not score, this chapter estimates a score on the same eight met-ocean criteria and weights; these are marked "est." and are judgements without an IBTrACS count. Base case: **70% political economy, 30% engineering.** Section 10 tests other weights.

**Governance data caveat.** The 2026 WGI release reports absolute 0 to 100 scores rather than percentile ranks, and warns against using them as definitive investment criteria.[^1] Small states rest on few sources: Kiribati's rule-of-law score uses 7, the Maldives' 8, Panama's 13.[^2]

## 4. Panama (Pacific side)

### 4.1 Legal hooks

- **Constitutional limits.** National territory, including the territorial sea, "may never be ceded, transferred or alienated, either temporarily or partially, to other States" (Art. 3). The territorial sea, its bed and subsoil belong to the State for public use and "may not be the object of private appropriation" (Art. 258). Concessions over waters must be "inspired by social welfare and public interest" (Art. 259). Retail trade is reserved for Panamanians (Art. 293).[^8] *Consequences:* sea-space can only ever be held by concession, never owned; the village shop and café need a Panamanian operator; and nothing may be framed as a cession to any state.
- **Special-zone precedent.** Law 41 of 2004 created the Panamá-Pacífico Special Economic Area with its own "legal, fiscal, customs, labour, immigration and business" regime applying to residents and visitors, run by an autonomous state agency that must select developers by an international selection process (Arts 1, 2 and 41).[^9] It was amended by Law 66 of 2018.[^10] This is the closest existing template for a zone statute.
- **City of Knowledge.** Decree-Law 6 of 1998 approved a contract between the State and a private foundation, transferring former Canal Zone property and granting tax exemptions for 25 years, renewable for further 25-year periods.[^11] It shows that Panama will contract with a foundation for a special-purpose enclave.
- **SEM regime.** Law 41 of 2007 (Multinational Headquarters) grants five-year renewable staff visas that need no work permit.[^12] It is useful for staff and residents' companies, not for tenure.
- **Tenure caps.** Maritime Authority (AMP) concessions run for at most 20 years, extendable once for up to the original term, and the AMP is not obliged to renew on the same terms (Law 56 of 2008, Art. 38).[^13] Public-private partnerships are capped at 30 years plus 10 and may only originate from the public sector (Law 93 of 2019, Arts 7 and 65); a 2026 reform bill would allow private initiatives and 40 plus 10 years, but is not law.[^14] **Neither reaches the 75 to 99 years the study needs; only a special statute can.**
- **Stability.** Law 54 of 1998 freezes the legal, national tax, customs and labour regime for 10 years for registered investments of at least US$2m, "save for reasons of public utility or social interest".[^15] Useful as a layer, too short and too qualified to carry the project alone.
- **Floating homes as vessels.** Any person, without nationality or domicile requirements, may register a vessel in Panama (Law 57 of 2008, Art. 3).[^16] Ocean Builders reports that its SeaPod gained "full legal approval as a houseboat vessel" (company claim, not independently verified); its pods are on the *Caribbean* coast near Puerto Lindo, not the Pacific.[^17] Vessel status is a practical route for a pilot module, but it brings flag-state safety rules and offers no self-government.

### 4.2 Governance and the court problem

Two Supreme Court rulings define the risk.

1. **Cobre Panamá (27 November 2023).** The full court unanimously declared Law 406 of 2023, which approved First Quantum's 20-year mining concession, unconstitutional.[^18] The court held that "contract-laws" are administrative acts subject to the Constitution and to procurement, environmental and fiscal law; that the concession was directly awarded without the required public tender; and that it breached some 25 articles, including Arts 258, 259 and 266.[^19] It noted that a 2017 ruling had already found the same defects.[^20] The mine has been shut for nearly three years; claims against Panama total at least US$27bn, and a government commission recommended in September 2026 negotiating a self-financed closure conditional on all arbitrations being dropped.[^21]
2. **Panama Ports Company (29 January 2026).** The court declared Law 5 of 1997, its addenda and the 2021 extension of CK Hutchison's Balboa and Cristóbal concession unconstitutional.[^22] The extension had never been countersigned by the Comptroller General nor approved by the National Assembly, and the court held that "no concession exists".[^23] Within a month the ruling was gazetted, PPC's workers were removed and interim operation passed to other operators, including Maersk.[^24]

The lesson is specific. Panama's courts do not strike concessions at random; they strike those that skipped competitive award, Comptroller countersignature, legislative approval or genuine public-interest justification. A seastead agreement that is directly negotiated, ratified as a contract-law and generous to foreigners would be exposed on all four counts.

**Stability.** The 2023 mining protests, the 2025 pension-reform protests (with a state of urgency, suspended guarantees and at least 376 arrests in Bocas del Toro)[^25] and a planned constituent assembly in 2027, with a new constitution intended to take effect on 1 January 2029,[^26] mean the constitutional ground will move during the concession's first years.

**Treaty cover is strong.** Panama is an ICSID member;[^7] the US-Panama Trade Promotion Agreement gives US investors ISDS under ICSID or UNCITRAL rules;[^27] and the 1983 UK-Panama BIT is in force with UNCITRAL arbitration.[^28] The Cobre Panamá claims show that treaty arbitration gives real leverage, and also, as with Próspera ([01 §4.8](01-legal.md)), that it converts a lost concession into a damages claim rather than restoring it.

### 4.3 Practicalities

- **Money and tax.** The US dollar circulates at par with the balboa. Income tax is territorial: foreign-source income is exempt for individuals and companies;[^29] from 2027 only foreign-source passive income of multinational-group entities without local substance is taxed at 15% (Law 526 of 2026).[^30]
- **Banking reputation.** Panama left the FATF grey list in October 2023 after a 2019 to 2023 listing (and an earlier 2014 to 2016 one).[^31] The EU removed it from its list of non-cooperative tax jurisdictions on 9 October 2026, but it stays on the monitored Annex II pending a 2027 review.[^5]
- **Residence.** The Friendly Nations route (51 countries) requires a local job, property of at least US$200,000, or a US$200,000 three-year deposit.[^32] The Qualified Investor route gives permanent residence for US$300,000 of first-sale property or US$500,000 of resale property (Executive Decree 17 of 2026).[^33] *Judgement:* a floating home registered as a vessel is unlikely to count as real property, so the deposit or employment routes are the safe ones; a legal opinion is needed.
- **Labour.** The 2026 minimum wage for construction in the Panama City region is US$3.51 an hour, about US$730 a month for a 48-hour week.[^34]
- **Logistics** are scored 5/5 in chapter 03 (Panama City port, yards, Tocumen airport), with the penalties a high tsunami rating and a 6.7 m spring tide.

### 4.4 Social licence

- **Indigenous waters are mostly on the other coast.** Guna Yala's Fundamental Law includes its islands, reefs and coastal sea on the Caribbean side and reserves all tourism there to Guna;[^35] foreign hotels on Guna islands in the 1960s were eventually burnt out or attacked.[^36] A Pacific site avoids comarca waters, which is a real point in Panama's favour over the Caribbean coast where Ocean Builders sits.
- **Fishers and conservation on the Pacific side.** The Pearl Islands form a 168,771 ha Special Marine-Coastal Management Zone under Law 18 of 2007, created to protect the livelihoods of communities that depend on its resources.[^37] The Gulf of Panama is the country's most important fishing ground (per the zone's management plan).[^37] Siting should avoid the zone and its satellite areas.
- **Mobilisation capacity.** The anti-mining movement brought down a ratified contract and the 2025 protests were partly about "the lack of free, prior and informed consultation" on extractive projects.[^25] Panama incorporated the Escazú Agreement on environmental participation by Law 125 of 2020, and the Cobre Panamá challengers relied on it.[^20]
- **Likely framing.** Panama's history with the Canal Zone makes "a foreign-run enclave on Panamanian water" a potent line, all the more after 2025's US pressure on the canal. Chapter 02's French Polynesia lesson applies directly: no site is named until consultation has begun.

### 4.5 Geopolitics

Panama let its Belt and Road agreement lapse in February 2025 after US Secretary of State Rubio warned of "measures" over Chinese influence near the canal.[^38] It is firmly in the US orbit, which suits American residents but also makes sovereignty a live domestic issue.

### 4.6 What Panama gets

Panama completed its first climate relocation in 2024, moving about 300 Guna families from Gardi Sugdub, and officials expect 63 coastal communities to need relocation in coming decades.[^39] A floating-housing demonstrator with open designs, a research partnership with the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute (already surveying the Pearl Islands),[^40] AMP concession fees, Panamanian construction and operating jobs, and diversification after the mine closure is a coherent offer. The risk is the optics of wealthy foreigners afloat while Guna families move inland; the offer must lead with the public benefit.

**Panama: Political economy 69/100; engineering 80; combined 72.**

## 5. Indonesia (Riau Islands)

### 5.1 Legal hooks

- **Special zones exist, but are economic only.** Law 39 of 2009 lets a private company propose a Special Economic Zone (KEK) and run it, with tax, customs, land, licensing and immigration facilities; foreigners in a KEK can hold stay permits of up to 5 years at a time, 15 years in total.[^41] The Riau Islands already have three KEKs (Nongsa Digital Park, Batam Aero Technic, Galang Batang).[^42] Batam, Rempang and Galang form a free-trade zone for 70 years from 2007, run by BP Batam.[^43] None of these devolves civil or commercial law.
- **Sea-space permit.** Any activity occupying sea space for 30 days or more needs a KKPRL (marine spatial-use conformity approval), issued via the OSS system and assessed against zoning plans.[^44] KKP's own technical guidance states that a KKPRL "cannot be transferred, cannot be assigned and cannot be used as security for debt", and that its validity follows the underlying business licence.[^45] *Consequence:* the tenure under the platform cannot be mortgaged or sold with the homes, which is a serious problem for residents' leaseholds and for any lender.
- **Constitutional limit.** In 2010 the Constitutional Court struck down the HP-3 coastal-water concession rights of Law 27 of 2007, holding that handing coastal waters to private capital would displace traditional fishers and customary communities and breach Article 33's "economic democracy".[^46] A long-term exclusive sea-space right for foreigners is exactly what that ruling forbade.
- **Foreign tenure on land.** Foreigners can hold *hak pakai* (right of use) for a house for 30 + 20 + 30 = 80 years over state land, and strata units only inside special and free-trade zones (Government Regulation 18 of 2021, Arts 49, 52, 71).[^47] At least 30% of any small island must stay under state control.[^48]
- **Floating structures** fall under "buildings and installations at sea" in the KKPRL process,[^45] not under a residential regime.

### 5.2 Governance, precedents and sovereignty sensitivity

- **Rempang (2023).** In September 2023 about 1,000 people clashed with police outside BP Batam over the planned eviction of Rempang's 7,500 residents (Malay, Orang Laut and Orang Darat communities present since the 17th century) for a Chinese-backed glass and solar plant; 43 were arrested.[^49] The project dropped off the 2025 national strategic project list,[^50] and BP Batam still describes it as Batam's only national strategic project in September 2026.[^51] This is the free-trade-zone authority a Riau seastead would deal with.
- **Islands are a sovereignty issue.** When four Anambas islands appeared on a foreign sales website in June 2025, ministers insisted islands cannot be sold, and the land minister said he suspected geopolitical motives.[^48][^52]
- **Unrest.** The August 2025 protests over lawmakers' perks spread to at least 32 of 38 provinces and were the worst violence in decades.[^53]
- **Treaties.** Indonesia began terminating its 60-plus BITs in 2014 (including those with the Netherlands, Singapore and China) after the Churchill Mining claim;[^54][^55][^58] it has no BIT with the US.[^56] The EU-Indonesia Investment Protection Agreement, concluded in 2025, contains no ISDS.[^57] The ASEAN Comprehensive Investment Agreement still offers ISDS to ASEAN investors,[^58] so a Singapore holding company is the natural structure.

### 5.3 Practicalities, social licence, geopolitics and offer

The second-home visa needs Rp2bn (about US$120,000) of funds and allows 5 or 10 years;[^59] the investor golden visa needs US$350,000 (5 years) or US$700,000 (10 years) in bonds, listed shares or funds.[^60] In practice the hospital and airport would be Singapore's (chapter 03 puts Batam's shipyard zone about 20 km away), which is an engineering strength and a political weakness: a foreign enclave on the Singapore side of Indonesian waters invites the "Singapore's annex" frame. Indonesia joined BRICS as a full member in January 2025.[^61] The offer to Indonesia is real (Batam's yards would build the caissons; a demonstrator for an archipelago facing sea-level rise), but it does not answer the tenure, the court or the sovereignty problem.

**Indonesia: Political economy 48/100; engineering 85; combined 59.**

## 6. Maldives (North Malé Atoll)

### 6.1 Legal hooks

The **Special Economic Zones Act 2014 was not repealed**; it was amended in November 2025.[^62] It overrides the Tourism, Land and Uninhabited Islands Acts for zone matters (s. 79), allows international arbitration clauses, grants "SEZ Residency" for the duration of a residential lease (s. 80), and lists, among incentives a developer may obtain by negotiation, land leases to foreign companies of 66 years plus a further 33 by supplementary agreement.[^63] The 2025 amendment created "sustainable townships" with a minimum investment of **US$500m**, 60% renewable energy and reduced income tax,[^64] three times this project's central cost. The Constitution, as amended in 2019, forbids foreign ownership of any territory and caps foreign leases at 99 years; the 2019 amendment reversed a 2015 change that had allowed foreign freehold for US$1bn reclamation projects, amid fears of Chinese military use.[^65] The Floating City developer describes a "legal framework and title deeds for the owners".[^66]

### 6.2 Governance and precedents

- **Contracts cancelled by the next government.** The Maldives cancelled GMR's 25-year Malé airport concession in 2012 after a change of government; a Singapore-seated UNCITRAL tribunal awarded about US$270m, paid in 2016.[^67] The award was honoured, but the concession was not.
- **Courts.** In May 2025 Parliament removed two Supreme Court justices who had been hearing a challenge to anti-defection amendments protecting the President's supermajority; the UN raised "serious concerns" about judicial independence.[^68]
- **Money.** Fitch rated the sovereign CCC- in June 2026 after it repaid a US$500m sukuk using most of its reserves;[^69] Moody's upgraded it to Caa1 in October 2026 but expects debt above 100% of GDP.[^70]
- **Treaties.** The Maldives is not an ICSID member.[^7]
- **Delivery.** The Floating City's prototypes are afloat, but its architect said in 2026 the project had "slowed down" ([01 §4.12](01-legal.md); [03 §1.11](03-site-and-structure.md)).[^66]

### 6.3 Practicalities, social licence and fit

There is no residence-by-investment law yet: the "Pearl Residence" scheme had no bill or published terms by October 2026, and Parliament rejected a motion to suspend it in August 2026.[^71] The existing corporate resident visa needs a US$250,000 five-year deposit or investment.[^72] **Civil-liberties fit is the worst in the field:** the Constitution makes Islam the state religion, citizens must be Muslim, non-Muslims may not worship publicly, and alcohol is sold to foreigners only on resort islands.[^73] India and China compete openly for influence: Indian troops were withdrawn in 2024,[^74] and India extended a US$565m credit line in 2025.[^75] The Maldives' motive (sea-level rise, housing) is the strongest in the field, but the Floating City already fills that niche.

**Maldives: Political economy 48/100; engineering 79; combined 57.**

## 7. El Salvador

- **Legal hooks.** A dollarised economy with a free-trade-zones law (reformed December 2025), a Special Regime for High-Value Investments (August 2025) and a Digital Assets Law (2023).[^76] No charter-city or sea-space concession law was found (unverified). "Bitcoin City", announced in 2021 near the Conchagua volcano, has had no groundbreaking.[^77]
- **Durability.** The governing party passed the IMF-required Bitcoin Law reform "minutes" after receiving it in January 2025, ending mandatory acceptance and tax payment in bitcoin, as a condition of a US$1.4bn IMF programme.[^78][^79] In July 2025 it approved indefinite presidential re-election and six-year terms in a single day.[^80] A legislature that can grant a charter overnight can revoke one overnight. The US State Department also reports a case in which criminal proceedings were used to revisit an arbitration already affirmed by a US court.[^76]
- **Treaties.** ICSID member and party to CAFTA-DR.[^76] In *Pac Rim v El Salvador* the CAFTA claims were dismissed under the denial-of-benefits clause because the claimant's US entity lacked substance, and the investor paid US$8m in costs.[^81] Treaty structuring must have real substance.
- **Residence.** The Freedom Visa costs **US$1m** in bitcoin or USDT (not US$5m) for a fast-track passport, capped at 1,000 a year.[^82]
- **Engineering (est. 53).** The obvious sheltered water, the Gulf of Fonseca, had 15 tropical storms within 300 km in chapter 03's IBTrACS count and is shared with Honduras and Nicaragua.
- **Fit.** Libertarian-friendly rhetoric, but a state of exception in force since March 2022;[^76] the "libertarians in a police state" frame is easy to write.

**El Salvador: Political economy 54/100; engineering 53 (est.); combined 54.**

## 8. Kiribati and São Tomé and Príncipe

- **Kiribati.** Native land "shall not be alienated, whether by sale, gift, lease or otherwise" to non-natives, and non-native leases need ministerial approval and island-court confirmation, with further approval needed beyond 99 years or 10 acres.[^83] In 2022 the government suspended every superior-court judge after rulings against it.[^84] It switched recognition from Taiwan to China in 2019 and reopened the Phoenix Islands Protected Area to commercial fishing despite UNESCO's concern.[^85][^86] It is not an ICSID member.[^7] Best weather in chapter 03, weakest legal base here. **Political economy 40; engineering 67; combined 48.**
- **São Tomé and Príncipe.** The president dismissed the prime minister and government by decree in January 2025, two years after a failed coup attempt.[^87] Reasonable CPI (45) and ICSID membership, but no lagoon and Gulf of Guinea piracy (chapter 03). **Political economy 49; engineering 58; combined 52.**

## 9. Candidates added during research

### 9.1 Malaysia (Johor)

Malaysia and Singapore signed the Johor-Singapore SEZ agreement on 7 January 2025, with 5% corporate tax for up to 15 years for qualifying activities and a 15% rate for knowledge workers;[^88] Forest City is a special financial zone inside it.[^89] MM2H residence has a dedicated SEZ/SFZ category with a 10-year renewable pass for a US$65,000 deposit (US$32,000 if aged 50 or over).[^90] Governance indices are better than Panama's (Section 3). Two warnings: in 2018 the then prime minister declared that foreigners would get no visas to live in Forest City because it was "built for foreigners, not built for Malaysians",[^91] and Forest City's 2014 reclamation began without the required environmental assessment and destroyed about 10 ha of seagrass, forcing a RM104m fishers' compensation fund.[^92][^93] Engineering is taken as equal to Riau (est. 85) because the Johor Strait shares its met-ocean setting. **Malaysia's sea-space tenure law and capacity to devolve rules were not researched here and are the first Stage 0 gap.** **Political economy 64; engineering 85 (est.); combined 70.**

### 9.2 United Arab Emirates

Dubai Law 3 of 2023 expressly empowers the Maritime Authority to "regulate the construction and use of floating houses, hotels, and structures" and register them as vessels;[^94] floating villas already sit off Dubai's World islands.[^95] Abu Dhabi Global Market applies English common law directly,[^96] which is the very model chapter 01 recommends. The 10-year golden residence needs AED 2m (about US$545,000) of property.[^97] Against that, the UAE scores lowest on voice and accountability (38), and in 2026 Gulf shipping, including ADNOC-operated vessels, has come under repeated attack, with the Joint Maritime Information Centre rating the threat "severe".[^98][^99] Engineering is estimated at 75 (very sheltered, but extreme water temperatures and the security criterion). **Political economy 69; engineering 75 (est.); combined 71.** If the Gulf conflict ends, the UAE moves to first place (Section 10); it fails the brief's libertarian purpose more than its engineering.

| Equal political-economy weights | Panama 73.8 | Malaysia 72.2 | UAE 67.6 | 4th |
| Ignore civil liberties and geopolitics | **UAE 76.9** | Panama 71.8 | Malaysia 70.8 | 5th |
| Gulf conflict ends (UAE D = 4, H = 3, engineering 81) | **UAE 76.8** | Panama 72.3 | Malaysia 70.3 | 4th |
| Another Panama annulment (Panama B = 1) | **UAE 70.8** | Malaysia 70.3 | Panama 69.5 | 4th |

**What the sensitivity shows.**

1. **The winner does change** under plausible weights: Malaysia if engineering carries more than 50% (it ties Panama at exactly 50%), the UAE if durability dominates or civil liberties are ignored. Panama wins outright in four of eleven scenarios, ties in two more, and is never below third.
2. **Indonesia never wins.** Its 5-point engineering lead over Panama is outweighed by a 21-point political-economy deficit; the crossover is at roughly 81% engineering weight, by which point Malaysia, in the same waters, is ahead anyway.
3. **A one-point change** on any 20%-weighted criterion moves a candidate by about 3 points. The top three should be treated as a tier, not a ranking.

**Does Panama hold up?** Yes, as first among near-equals, and on different grounds from the synthesis. The synthesis cited vessel approval, the ship registry, the dollar and cyclone-free water. Its real weakness, which the synthesis did not mention, is that its Supreme Court has twice voided ratified concessions of exactly the type chapter 01 recommends.

## Verdict

> **Overall: AMBER.** A suitable host exists, but none offers a durable, self-governing sea-space concession off the shelf. Panama is the recommended lead (72/100), with Malaysia (Johor) as the parallel fallback and the UAE on a watch list; Riau's engineering lead does not survive the political-economy test.
>
> | Host | Rating | One-line reason |
> |---|---|---|
> | Panama (Pacific) | **Amber** | Best all-round, but its Supreme Court voids ratified concessions that skip tender, countersignature or consultation |
> | Malaysia (Johor) | **Amber** | Excellent water and governance; tenure law unresearched; Forest City shows how quickly policy can turn |
> | UAE | **Amber/Red** | Floating-house statute and common-law zone, but an active Gulf conflict and poor civil-liberties fit |
> | Indonesia (Riau) | **Red as host**, Green as builder | Non-mortgageable sea permit, 2010 court ruling against private coastal rights, Rempang |
> | Maldives | **Red** for this brief | Islamic constitution, judicial purge, not ICSID, CCC- sovereign |
> | El Salvador, Kiribati, São Tomé | **Red** | Cyclones, institutions or logistics |
>
> **Top five host-specific risks**
> 1. **Constitutional annulment in Panama** (Cobre Panamá 2023, Panama Ports 2026). *Mitigation:* framework statute, competitive award, Comptroller countersignature, Escazú consultation, compensation-on-invalidation clause.
> 2. **Constitutional change in Panama, 2027 to 2029.** *Mitigation:* pilot under ordinary law; legislate after the new constitution settles.
> 3. **Social-licence failure** (Gulf of Panama fishers, environmental movement, "new Canal Zone" framing). *Mitigation:* consult before naming the site; benefit fund; public access; Panamanian operators.
> 4. **Tenure shorter than the hull.** Panama's AMP cap is 20 plus 20 years; Indonesia's KKPRL cannot be mortgaged. *Mitigation:* only a statute can deliver 75 to 99 years; do not commit Phase 1 capital before it exists.
> 5. **Weak treaty structuring.** *Mitigation:* a US entity with real substance (Pac Rim's lesson) and political-risk insurance; remember that arbitration compensates but does not restore.
