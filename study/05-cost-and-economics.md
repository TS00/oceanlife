# 05. Cost, financing and economics

## Summary

- **Phase 1 capital cost (CAPEX), sheltered host-nation lagoon: about US$184 million central (range US$76m to US$458m) in 2026 dollars.** That is about **US$7.4m per family** (US$3.0m to US$18.3m), or about **US$9,200 per m² of platform**. Once the cost is adjusted for the overrun record of first-of-a-kind projects, the expected outturn is nearer **US$245m (about US$9.8m per family)**.
- **The marine works dominate.** Platform, breakwater, moorings and connectors make up about 63% of direct cost in the central case. The biggest single cost driver is site exposure: moving from a reef-sheltered lagoon to an exposed site in a host state's exclusive economic zone (EEZ) multiplies total cost by roughly **2.5 to 3.5 times**.
- **Running costs (OPEX) are about US$6.5m a year central, or about US$21,500 per family per month before community revenue** (about US$19,200 after it). That is roughly 160 times the median US homeowners' association (HOA) fee, and it sits at the low end of what residents of *MS The World* pay.
- **No bank will fund the construction of the platform.** Debt exists for floating homes and vessels, but lenders want a berth or lease granted by a public body, an insured hull and a resale market. In practice the project has to be **equity-led**: founders' capital, staged against decision gates, plus a standby capital call and a strategic industrial partner. Debt is limited to revenue-earning assets once they are operating.
- **The community cannot pay its own way.** Plausible community businesses (guest rooms, events, a research station, visiting yachts, aquaculture) cover only about 10 to 20% of OPEX. The residents' outside wealth and remote-work income have to carry the rest. In practice that means founders with a net worth of around US$20 to 40m each.
- **The "is it worth it" test:** a comparable village on land in Panama or Portugal costs about **US$1.1 to 1.2m per family**, roughly one-sixth of the floating central case. A three-bedroom apartment on *MS The World* costs a similar amount up front but about twice as much over 20 years. The premium buys autonomy, not amenity, and the autonomy is only as durable as the host-state agreement.

**Verdict: Amber.** The project is financeable only as a wealthy founders' venture at a sheltered host-nation site, and only through a staged pilot. A high-seas site is economically a Red (see the Verdict box).

**The "$167m" figure.** The Independent's 2017 report that the French Polynesia project "is projected to cost $167 million"[^9] matches the 2013 DeltaSync estimate for an 11-platform village ("just over $167 million"[^7]). It does not match Blue Frontiers' own pilot budget, which TSI gave as US$10m to 50m[^10] and NBC as about US$60m.[^11] We treat $167m as the DeltaSync number.

**What the DeltaSync breakdown shows.** The estimate before fees was €88.6m. Of this, €38.9m was concrete platforms (built up from concrete at €1,400/m³ in place), €4.4m was "maritime constructions" (moorings at €37,500 each, connectors and bridges) and €39.6m was buildings at €1,000/m² of gross floor area. A further 25% was added for development costs (fees, financing) and 12% for Honduran VAT.[^6] DeltaSync designed only for protected bays "since the costs of a breakwater are prohibitively expensive".[^8]

**Oceanix** shows the overrun pattern before any concrete was poured. The headline estimate went from US$200m (2021)[^14] to US$220m[^15] to a reported US$600m (2025), with the city saying it had not been shown a financing plan.[^16] Oceanix's own view was that a prototype "is something venture capital does".[^15]

### 2.7 Synthesis

On a cost-per-m²-of-deck ladder in 2026 USD, the benchmarks fall into four bands. A sheltered concrete hull costs about $700 to $2,200/m². A steel VLFS hull costs about $1,800 to $2,400/m². Floating towns with buildings come in at roughly $4,000 (Oceanix's early estimate) to $10,000/m² (Blue Frontiers, Oceanix's later figure, Schoonschip). Open-ocean semi-submersibles cost $12,000 to $15,000/m². No benchmark has been built at the scale of this project and then audited. Every whole-town figure above is a pre-construction estimate, and the two with revision histories (Oceanix and Schoonschip) rose by 2 to 3 times.

## 5. Financing

### 5.1 Options

**Founders' equity.** At the central cost, 25 families would each fund **US$7.4m**, or about US$9.8m on a risk-adjusted basis. For comparison, *MS The World* requires a US$10m net worth or US$1m of annual post-tax income to buy residences priced at US$3.5m to 15m.[^29][^32] A similar test here implies founders worth **US$20 to 40m** each. That is a very small market of high-net-worth individuals (HNWIs) who are also willing to relocate.

**Pre-sales.** These are useful for Phase 2 plots and hotel keys. Off-plan purchasers will demand escrow and step-in rights. Ocean Builders auctioned 777 *Satoshi* cabins from US$25k and sold "only a handful",[^34] so demand from ideological buyers below HNWI level is not bankable evidence.

**Debt.** Marine mortgages do exist:
- *Dutch floating homes.* Triodos lends up to 90% of market value on ship-registered water homes with concrete hulls built since 2012.[^62] Orange Credit requires a fixed berth permit, a concrete or steel hull, sinking and storm cover, and caps loans at 80 to 90% of value.[^64] Since 1 January 2026 the national mortgage guarantee (NHG) covers floating homes, but only where the berth is leased or permitted **by a government body** and is freely transferable.[^63]
- *Yachts.* Rates in 2026 run about 6.5 to 8.5% for new vessels with 20% down, and 8.5 to 12%+ for specialist cases. Foreign flag or live-aboard use adds 0.5 to 2 points.[^65][^66]

The pattern is consistent. Lenders want a **public-law berth right, a classed and insured hull, and a resale market**. A libertarian special zone weakens the first, and the *Satoshi* and SeaPod experience undermines the second.[^28][^33] Construction debt for the platform should therefore be assumed **unavailable**. Post-completion debt on revenue-earning assets (hotel, marina, utilities) may become available after 2 to 3 years of operating history.

**Host-state or development finance.** Oceanix framed Busan as climate adaptation under UN-Habitat,[^13] and the Maldives project is a public–private partnership with the government.[^17] A libertarian polity is unlikely to receive multilateral lending. The realistic host contribution is **in kind**: a long seabed lease at a low fee, permits, and port access.

**Philanthropy and venture capital.** Peter Thiel's founding pledge to TSI was US$500k (2008), spent mainly on design studies such as ClubStead.[^67][^40] Seasteading has attracted research money, not construction capital. Venture investors may back a **platform-building company** with intellectual property and a pipeline. They will not back a 25-house town whose return is the residents' freedom.

**Tokenisation and crypto.** Blue Frontiers raised 3,100 ETH in a 2018 presale for its Varyon token, then postponed the main sale and refunded buyers. It cited the crypto bear market and slipping milestones.[^68] TSI blames a crypto crash in part for the project's collapse.[^12] Under the US *Howey* test the SEC treats most investment-style tokens as securities,[^69] and in the EU, MiCA has applied in full since 30 December 2024, with white-paper, authorisation and liability rules.[^70] A public token sale adds regulatory risk and price volatility while delivering no cheaper capital. A **private, KYC-gated digital share register** is acceptable for record-keeping.

### 7.3 Scale and interest rate

**Scale (75 families, Phase 2).** Platform area triples, but breakwater length grows only with the square root of area (about 1.73 times), and the school, clinic and fixed staff do not triple. Central direct cost comes to about US$284m. With 16.5% fees and a 25% "second-of-a-kind" contingency, the total is about **US$414m, or US$5.5m per family**. That is **about 25% less per family**, not the two-thirds that scale advocates imply (DeltaSync's breakwater arithmetic assumed thousands of homes[^6]).

**Reading the table.** On cost alone, every land option beats the seastead by roughly 3 to 10 times, depending on the option and the time horizon. *MS The World* is the only alternative in the same price class, and a family-sized residence there costs about twice as much over 20 years. The seastead's justification is therefore **non-financial**: a degree of self-government that none of the land options offers. The Próspera case shows how fragile special-zone autonomy is; the legal chapter must say whether a floating zone is any less fragile. A floating town can in principle be towed away from a hostile host, which is the one hedge land cannot offer. But towing a 20,000 m² town with its breakwater is a multi-million-dollar marine operation, not a quick exit (the ocean-tow benchmark alone is about US$1m per unit per 1,000 miles).[^38]

## 9. Risk-adjusted view and staging

### 9.1 Reference-class evidence

In Flyvbjerg's database of over 16,000 projects, only **47.9% come in on budget or better, 8.5% on budget and on time, and 0.5% on budget, on time and with the promised benefits**.[^81][^82] Overruns follow fat-tailed distributions, and "overruns of up to 50 percent in real terms are common, over 50 percent not uncommon".[^80] For the "buildings" class, published summaries of his 2023 appendix give a **mean overrun of +62%**, with 39% of projects overrunning by 50% or more and a mean overrun of +206% within that tail. These figures come from secondary summaries and have not been checked against the book.[^81] The floating-city record fits this pattern. Oceanix's budget tripled before construction.[^16] Schoonschip's cheapest home went from €110k to €250k.[^21] Freedom Ship went from US$6bn to US$15 to 20bn.[^35] The Maldives Floating City completion date has moved to 2028.[^18]

### 9.2 Probability-weighted outturn

The scenarios are applied to the pre-contingency base of US$139m. The weights are this author's judgement, calibrated to the reference class above.

| Scenario | Probability | Outturn factor on base | Outturn (US$m) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Disciplined delivery | 35% | ×1.15 | 160 |
| Typical overrun | 40% | ×1.50 | 209 |
| Fat tail (redesign, insurance or permit shock, storm damage during build) | 25% | ×3.00 | 417 |
| **Probability-weighted expected cost** | | ×1.75 | **~244** |

The expected overrun (+75%) is a little above Flyvbjerg's buildings mean (+62%). Under these weights the central estimate of US$184m has about a **65% chance of being exceeded**. A prudent founder should plan on **about US$9.8m per family**, with a pre-agreed stop or de-scope rule if the fat tail appears.

### 9.3 Staged plan with gates

Flyvbjerg's remedy is to "think slow, act fast", using modularity to shrink both the tail and the exposure window.[^82][^84]

| Stage | Scope | Indicative cost | Gate to proceed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Studies (12 to 18 months) | Site metocean data, basin model tests, class pre-approval, host agreement term sheet, insurance quotes | $2m to $4m | Binding host lease; written quotes from insurers for hull and liability; P80 estimate below founders' committed capacity |
| 1. Pilot module (24 months) | One 50 × 50 m module (2,500 m²), 2 to 3 homes, utility core, 150 m attenuator, one vessel | **$15m to $25m** | One full wet season survived within motion criteria; measured maintenance cost; insurance renewed; a mortgage offer obtained on at least one home |
| 2. Phase 1a | 4 to 5 modules, 12 homes, communal core | ~45% of Phase 1 | Outturn within +25% of the gate-1 estimate |
| 3. Phase 1b | Complete 20,000 m², 25 homes | Balance | As above |

The pilot costs about 10% of the Phase 1 central estimate. Its purpose is to buy information: whether the town is insurable, bankable, legally stable and physically sound. Those are the questions that killed *Satoshi*,[^33] Blue Frontiers[^12] and the Thai seastead[^83] before any economics mattered.

## 10. Assumptions in the design basis that should be revised

1. **The 20,000 m² deck is the main cost lever, and it is generous.** At 800 m² of floating deck per family, every m² costs about US$2,800 once fees and contingency are added, before the breakwater. Cutting open space from 5,000 to 2,500 m², food production from 2,000 to 1,000 m² and circulation by 500 m² gives about **14,000 m²** of deck. That saves about 30% of platform area, shortens the breakwater by about 16% and cuts total CAPEX by about **US$30m (16%)**. Recommendation: make 14,000 m² the Phase 1 target and treat 20,000 m² as an option.
2. **Drop the high-seas archetype for Phase 1.** At 3.5 to 5 times the cost, with no insurance or debt market, it is economically a non-starter. Archetype 1 should be the base case. Archetype 2 should be a later option, conditional on the pilot results.
3. **The population and finance assumptions are inconsistent.** Utilities for 150 people and 10 to 15 staff are reasonable. But the resulting cost of about US$19k per family per month (net) plus US$7 to 10m of capital means the founders are HNWIs, not a broad libertarian membership. Governance and demand chapters should plan for that demographic, or the brief should accept a denser and cheaper design (for example 40 smaller homes on 12,000 m²).
4. **"Fully solar" is not a cost problem.** Energy is under 3% of CAPEX. The solar brief is affordable; the breakwater is not.
5. **Gardens on deck need a load allowance.** Soil at roughly 1.5 to 2 t/m² over 4,500 m² of gardens and orchard beds drives platform depth and cost. The systems and structure chapters should specify container or hydroponic planting, or the platform unit cost should move to the high case.

## Verdict

> **AMBER: feasible with significant caveats, but only for a sheltered host-nation site funded by wealthy founders through a staged pilot. A high-seas site is economically RED.**
>
> - Phase 1 CAPEX of **US$184m central (US$76m to US$458m)**, or **US$7.4m per family**; risk-adjusted expectation about **US$245m (US$9.8m per family)**.
> - OPEX of about **US$6.5m a year**, or **about US$21,500 per family per month gross** (about US$19,200 net of community revenue): about 160 times a median HOA fee, and comparable to *MS The World*.
> - Financing is **equity-led** (about 70% from founders including standby capital, about 10% from a strategic partner, with debt only after completion). No public token sale.
> - Land alternatives cost roughly **one-sixth as much**. The premium buys autonomy, which is only as durable as the host-state agreement.
>
> **Top risks**
> 1. **Fat-tailed cost overrun.** First-of-a-kind marine works; every floating-city estimate with a revision history has risen by 2 to 3 times.
> 2. **Insurability and bankability.** No insurer has yet been shown to cover an inhabited, polity-style floating town; lenders require public-law berth rights.
> 3. **Host-state political or legal reversal.** The Próspera ZEDE ruling, the end of Blue Frontiers and the Thai raid all show how quickly protection can disappear, stranding an asset that is expensive to move.
> 4. **Site exposure and breakwater cost underestimated.** This is the single largest swing (+US$112m to +US$430m).
> 5. **Founder concentration and illiquidity.** With 25 payers, every departure raises the others' levy, and the resale market is unproven.
