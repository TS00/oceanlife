# 01. Legal status and sovereignty

## Summary

International law leaves no room for a new sovereign country made of floating platforms. An artificial structure is not an island and generates no territory or maritime zones (UNCLOS Arts 60(8) and 121(1)). No entity may claim sovereignty over any part of the high seas (Art 89). A coastal state has *exclusive* jurisdiction over artificial islands and installations in its exclusive economic zone (Arts 60 and 80). The only court to have ruled directly on the point, the Administrative Court of Cologne in the Sealand case (1978), held that a man-made platform cannot be state territory. Every attempt since 1968 to declare sovereignty offshore has ended in one of three ways: forcible removal (Rose Island, Minerva, the Thai seapod), quiet tolerance as a curiosity (Sealand), or collapse before launch (Operation Atlantis, Blue Frontiers, MS Satoshi).

What *is* achievable is substantial **autonomy under a host state's sovereignty**: a long-term concession of sheltered water inside a cooperating state's territorial sea, a special economic zone statute that hands civil and commercial law, taxation and much regulation to the community, international arbitration and stabilisation protections, and an internal constitution built from private law (a foundation, leases and a residents' covenant). That is the recommended pathway. Even this is politically fragile, as Próspera in Honduras and the Seasteading Institute in French Polynesia both showed. It should be designed on the assumption that the host's politics will change during the platform's 50-year life.

**Verdict: Amber** for an autonomous floating town under a host-state charter. **Red** for anything presented, or behaving, as a sovereign country.

## 1. The law of the sea: where the seastead could sit

The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS, 1982) divides the ocean into zones with very different rules. The seastead's legal character changes completely depending on which zone it is in.

| Zone | Breadth (from baselines) | Coastal state's power | What it means for a seastead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Internal waters (lagoons, bays, ports) | Landward of baselines | Full sovereignty, as on land (Art 8) | Host law applies fully; best physical shelter |
| Territorial sea | Up to 12 nm (Art 3) | Sovereignty, subject to innocent passage (Arts 2, 17) | Host law applies fully; a moored platform is not "in passage" |
| Contiguous zone | Up to 24 nm (Art 33) | Customs, fiscal, immigration and sanitary enforcement | Host can police supply runs and arrivals |
| Exclusive economic zone (EEZ) | Up to 200 nm (Art 57) | Sovereign rights over resources; **exclusive** jurisdiction over artificial islands and installations (Arts 56, 60) | Host consent required; host law governs the structure |
| Continental shelf | 200 nm or further to the margin edge (Art 76) | Sovereign rights over the seabed; Art 60 applies mutatis mutandis (Art 80) | Any seabed-anchored structure is under host jurisdiction even beyond 200 nm where the shelf extends |
| High seas | Beyond the EEZ (Art 86) | None; freedoms of all states (Art 87) | Flag-state law for ships; no sovereignty possible (Art 89) |

Sources: UNCLOS Parts II, V, VI and VII.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

### 1.1 Territorial sea and contiguous zone

Within 12 nautical miles the coastal state is sovereign over the water, the seabed and the airspace (Art 2).[^1] Foreign ships enjoy only a right of innocent passage, which must be "continuous and expeditious" (Arts 17 and 18). A floating town permanently moored in a host's territorial sea is therefore, legally, on host territory for most practical purposes. In the contiguous zone (to 24 nm, Art 33) the host can enforce customs, fiscal, immigration and sanitary laws, which catches every supply boat and visitor.[^1]

### 1.2 EEZ and continental shelf: Articles 56, 60 and 80

The EEZ is not "international waters" for a structure. Article 56(1)(b)(i) gives the coastal state jurisdiction over "the establishment and use of artificial islands, installations and structures". Article 60 then makes this concrete:[^2]

- **Art 60(1):** the coastal state has "the exclusive right to construct and to authorize and regulate the construction, operation and use of" (a) artificial islands, (b) installations and structures for economic purposes, and (c) installations and structures "which may interfere with the exercise of the rights of the coastal State in the zone". A residential platform with a marina, fishing and desalination outfalls falls within (a) or (c) on any sensible reading.
- **Art 60(2):** the coastal state "shall have exclusive jurisdiction over such artificial islands, installations and structures, including jurisdiction with regard to customs, fiscal, health, safety and immigration laws and regulations".
- **Art 60(3):** due notice and permanent warning of presence are required, and abandoned structures must be removed.
- **Art 60(4) and (5):** safety zones of up to 500 m may be declared around them.
- **Art 60(7):** they may not be placed where they interfere with recognised sea lanes essential to international navigation.

Article 80 applies Article 60 "mutatis mutandis" to the continental shelf.[^3] Coastal states use this power in full. The US Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends "the Constitution and laws and civil and political jurisdiction of the United States" to "all artificial islands on the outer Continental Shelf", and adopts the adjacent state's civil and criminal law as federal law there.[^5]

**Consequence:** moving from the territorial sea into the EEZ does *not* buy legal autonomy. The host's jurisdiction over the structure is the same in kind and only slightly narrower in scope. The design basis assumes that the "host-nation EEZ" setting gives "more autonomy" than the sheltered territorial-sea setting. Legally, that assumption is wrong (see Section 9).

### 1.3 High seas: Articles 87, 89, 91 to 94 and 110

Beyond national jurisdiction, Article 87(1)(d) lists among the freedoms of the high seas the "freedom to construct artificial islands and other installations permitted under international law".[^4] Three qualifications matter:

1. **The freedom belongs to states, not individuals.** Article 87 says the high seas are "open to all States" and the freedoms are exercised "both for coastal and land-locked States". Private persons exercise them through a state, by flagging a ship or registering an installation. Article 109(3)(b) even refers to "the State of registry of the installation", which assumes that installations on the high seas have one.[^4]
2. **No sovereignty.** Article 89: "No State may validly purport to subject any part of the high seas to its sovereignty." A new entity claiming the water around its platform is making exactly the claim that Article 89 forbids.[^4]
3. **Freedoms must be exercised "with due regard for the interests of other States"** (Art 87(2)).[^4]

For ships, Article 91 lets each state fix the conditions for granting its nationality, provided there is "a genuine link between the State and the ship". Article 92(1) puts a ship under the "exclusive jurisdiction" of its single flag state on the high seas. Article 92(2) treats a ship that uses two flags "according to convenience" as a ship without nationality. Article 94 obliges the flag state to exercise "effective jurisdiction and control in administrative, technical and social matters", including construction, manning, surveys and safety equipment.[^4]

### 1.4 Artificial islands are not islands: Articles 121 and 60(8)

Article 121(1) defines an island as "a naturally formed area of land, surrounded by water, which is above water at high tide".[^6] Article 60(8) is categorical: "Artificial islands, installations and structures do not possess the status of islands. They have no territorial sea of their own, and their presence does not affect the delimitation of the territorial sea, the exclusive economic zone or the continental shelf."[^2] Even a recognised state that built a floating city could not use it to generate maritime zones. A non-state certainly cannot.

### 1.5 Stateless vessels, the right of visit and piracy

Article 110(1) lets a warship board a foreign ship on the high seas only on reasonable suspicion of piracy, slave trading, unauthorised broadcasting, false flag, or that "the ship is without nationality" (Art 110(1)(d)).[^4] A seastead that refuses any flag invites boarding by any navy.

The consequences of statelessness go beyond boarding. In *Molvan v Attorney-General for Palestine* (1948) the Privy Council upheld the forfeiture of the *Asya*, a vessel with no valid flag, holding that such a vessel enjoys no protection of any state on the high seas.[^7] US law treats "a vessel without nationality" as "subject to the jurisdiction of the United States" for drug enforcement (46 USC 70502(c)(1)(A)). It defines statelessness to include failing, on request, to make a claim of nationality (s 70502(d)).[^8] Under 18 USC 7, US "special maritime and territorial jurisdiction" covers "any vessel belonging in whole or in part to ... any citizen" of the US on the high seas, and "any place outside the jurisdiction of any nation with respect to an offense by or against a national of the United States".[^9] A high-seas seastead with American owners or residents is not beyond US criminal law. It is explicitly inside it.

**Piracy** (Art 101) requires "illegal acts of violence or detention, or any act of depredation, committed for private ends" against another ship or persons.[^4] A peaceful seastead is not a pirate vessel. But armed resistance to an inspection party could change that characterisation. Separately, **unauthorised broadcasting** from an installation on the high seas (Art 109) can be prosecuted by the flag state, the "State of registry of the installation", the broadcaster's state of nationality, or any state that can receive the transmissions or suffers interference. Those states may arrest the persons and seize the equipment.[^4] Sealand and Rose Island both began with broadcasting ambitions. A seastead running a public radio service or an unlicensed wireless network that spills ashore would be exposed.

## 2. Statehood

### 2.1 The Montevideo criteria

Article 1 of the Montevideo Convention on the Rights and Duties of States (1933) is the standard statement of statehood: "a) a permanent population; b) a defined territory; c) government; and d) capacity to enter into relations with the other states."[^10] Applied to this seastead:

| Criterion | Seastead position | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Permanent population | 100 to 150 people, rising to ~400 | Small but not disqualifying in principle. The Sealand court said size is irrelevant, but it required a "cohesive vibrant community" rather than a commercial or tax association.[^11] A community recruited partly for tax reasons is vulnerable to the same objection. |
| Defined territory | A floating, movable, man-made hull | **Fails.** The Cologne court held that territory "must consist in a natural segment of the earth's surface", and that an artificial island, "albeit connected to the earth's surface", does not qualify.[^11] A floating platform that is not even fixed to the seabed is weaker still. UNCLOS Arts 60(8) and 121(1) point the same way. |
| Government | A residents' assembly or council | Achievable |
| Capacity for relations | Depends on other states being willing to deal with it | In practice, a function of recognition |

### 2.2 Declaratory versus constitutive recognition

Article 3 of the Montevideo Convention adopts the **declaratory** view: "The political existence of the state is independent of recognition by the other states."[^10] The EC Arbitration Commission on Yugoslavia (the Badinter Commission) put it the same way in Opinion No. 1 (1991): "the existence or disappearance of the state is a question of fact; ... the effects of recognition by other states are purely declaratory".[^12] The **constitutive** view holds that an entity becomes a state, with rights others must respect, only when other states recognise it.

The debate matters less than it appears. Under the declaratory view the seastead still has to *meet* the criteria as a matter of fact, and it fails on territory. Under the constitutive view it needs recognition, and no state has ever recognised a micronation built on an artificial platform. Either way the result is the same.

### 2.3 What recognition requires in practice

- **Bilateral recognition** is a political act by each state's executive. Existing states have consistently refused to recognise offshore micronations, and neighbouring states have actively opposed them (Tonga and the South Pacific Forum over Minerva; Croatia and Serbia over Liberland; see Section 4).
- **UN membership** under Article 4 of the UN Charter is open to "peace-loving states" and requires "a decision of the General Assembly upon the recommendation of the Security Council".[^13] That means nine Security Council votes, no veto from any permanent member, and then a two-thirds vote in the General Assembly. For a tax-motivated platform of 150 people, the realistic probability is zero.
- **Functional recognition**, the thing that actually matters day to day, means: passports accepted at borders, a ship registry other states respect, ITU frequency assignments, correspondent banking, and a seat in treaty systems such as ICAO, IMO, the Universal Postal Union and the OECD tax-exchange networks. Each of these depends on statehood or on a sovereign sponsor.

### 2.4 A useful distinction: sea-level-rise statehood

The 2023 Pacific Islands Forum Declaration on the Continuity of Statehood affirms "a general presumption that a State, once established, will continue to exist", even if climate change submerges its land.[^14] Some seasteading advocates cite this as evidence that territory is no longer essential. It is not. The Declaration protects *existing, recognised* states against losing statehood. It does not create a route for new entities to acquire it. If anything it reinforces the point that the only realistic way to be "sovereign at sea" is to be part of a state that already exists.

## 3. Ship or structure?

The seastead's legal personality depends on whether it is a vessel (flagged, with flag-state law aboard) or an installation (governed by the law of the coastal state or of a state of registry).

| Issue | Flagged vessel | Moored installation in host waters or EEZ |
|---|---|---|
| Governing law aboard | Flag state's law on the high seas (Art 92); coastal state's law when in its territorial sea or ports | Host law (sovereignty in territorial sea; Art 60(2) in the EEZ) |
| Safety regime | SOLAS, Load Lines, STCW, MLC, MARPOL via the flag state | Host building codes plus classification rules; MARPOL still applies to "fixed or floating platforms" |
| Passenger status of residents | Residents are "passengers": SOLAS passenger-ship rules apply above 12 passengers on international voyages | Not passengers; normal residential occupancy |
| Insurance | P&I (third-party liability) plus hull and machinery; must satisfy flag and class | Property, liability and marine-construction cover; P&I only if vessel-like |
| Mobility | Can relocate (the "exit option") | Moored; relocation needs host consent and planning |
| Main precedent | MS The World (works); MS Satoshi (failed) | Maldives Floating City, Oceanix Busan, Ocean Builders SeaPods in Panama |

### 3.1 The SOLAS problem

SOLAS Chapter I Regulation 1 applies the Convention "only to ships engaged on international voyages", and Regulation 2 defines a passenger ship as one that "carries more than twelve passengers". A "passenger" is anyone who is not crew or "engaged ... on the business of that ship", or a child under one.[^15] Residents and their children are therefore passengers. The terms of carriage of MS *The World*, a Bahamas-flagged residential ship, define "Passenger(s)" to include "Residents".[^16] A 2-hectare floating garden town with houses, trees and a pool would struggle to meet passenger-ship rules on subdivision, fire protection, stability and lifesaving appliances. Those rules were written for cruise ships, not villages. A vessel that never makes an international voyage escapes SOLAS itself, but then it sits in one state's waters under that state's domestic rules, which defeats the purpose of flagging.

MARPOL cannot be avoided whatever the label. Article 2(4) defines "ship" to include "floating craft and fixed or floating platforms".[^17]

### 3.2 Flags of convenience

Open registries dominate world shipping. On 1 January 2025 Liberia (17.4%), Panama (15.2%) and the Marshall Islands (12.5%) together held 46.5% of world deadweight tonnage.[^18] The International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea held in *M/V Saiga (No. 2)* (1999) that the genuine-link requirement exists "to secure more effective implementation of the duties of the flag State, and not to establish criteria by reference to which the validity of the registration of ships in a flag State may be challenged by other States" (para 83).[^19] Other states therefore cannot easily refuse to recognise a flag of convenience.

The flag route has three fatal weaknesses for this brief:

1. **The flag state is sovereign aboard.** Flagging in Panama or the Marshall Islands puts residents under Panamanian or Marshallese law on the high seas. This is a change of landlord, not independence.
2. **The flag state applies full seagoing rules.** In 2020 Ocean Builders asked the Panama Maritime Authority to treat the former cruise ship *Satoshi* as a non-seagoing vessel. Panama replied that "because we have an engine and we have a flag, we are to be considered seagoing".[^20]
3. **Flags can be withdrawn.** Registration is a privilege the flag state can revoke. Once deregistered, the vessel is stateless (Section 1.5).

### 3.3 Insurance as the real gatekeeper

The *Satoshi* project died on insurance, not law. After buying a 1991 cruise ship, completing a class drydocking and sailing it towards Panama, Ocean Builders could not find an International Group P&I club willing to cover a stationary residential ship. The closest offer was "a million dollar premium for a maximum of $5 million in coverage". The ship was sold for scrap in December 2020.[^20] Without P&I, "it cannot have a crew, which means the ship would lose its class rating and flag".[^21] The *Guardian*'s reconstruction adds that Panama would not let the ship discharge treated wastewater locally, so it would have had to sail 12 miles out every 20 days or so to empty its tanks. That kind of obstacle made it "an off-putting proposition for insurers".[^22] The twelve International Group clubs cover about 90% of the world's ocean-going tonnage,[^23] so their appetite effectively decides whether a novel floating structure can operate as a ship.

**Conclusion on classification:** treat the main platform as a **moored floating installation classed by a classification society** (DNV, ABS or Bureau Veritas rules for floating structures, as the design basis already assumes) and sited in host waters. Treat the ferries, tenders and any detachable modules as ordinary flagged small craft.

## 4. Precedents

| Case | Years | Location and zone | What happened | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Principality of Sealand | 1967 to date | WWII fort, Thames estuary; then outside the UK's 3-nm sea, now inside its 12-nm sea | 1968 English court lacked jurisdiction; 1978 German court held it is not a state; UK extended territorial sea in 1987 | Survives by tolerance, not law |
| Republic of Rose Island | 1968 to 1969 | 400 m² platform off Rimini, just outside Italy's then territorial sea | Occupied by Italian police 55 days after declaring independence; demolished by Italian Navy explosives | Coastal states act fast against nearby platforms |
| Republic of Minerva | 1972 | Reefs south of Fiji, unclaimed at the time | Tonga built islets, proclaimed sovereignty over a 12-mile radius and removed the settlers; regional states backed Tonga | Neighbours will claim "empty" places rather than allow a micronation |
| Operation Atlantis | 1968 to 1970s | Caribbean (Silver Shoals) | Ferro-cement ship lost in a storm; later island-building attempt chased off by gunboats | Engineering failure comes first; then local states |
| Blue Frontiers / French Polynesia | 2017 to 2018 | Tahiti lagoon (territorial waters) | Non-binding MoU expired; local electoral opposition; government disowned it | An MoU is not a concession; social licence is decisive |
| Ocean Builders seapod, Thailand | 2019 | 14 nm off Phuket (Thai EEZ) | Navy seized it; founders charged in absentia with violating sovereignty, punishable by death or life imprisonment | The EEZ is not "international waters" for structures |
| Ocean Builders, Panama | 2020 to date | Linton Bay, ~1.2 km offshore | SeaPods approved as houseboat vessels under Panamanian law | Works, because it is fully inside host law |
| MS Satoshi | 2020 to 2021 | Gulf of Panama (planned) | Failed on P&I insurance and seagoing classification; scrapped, then resold | Ship status brings the full maritime regime |
| Próspera ZEDE, Honduras | 2020 to date | Roatán (land) | ZEDE law repealed 2022 and declared unconstitutional 2024; ICSID claim reduced from $10.775bn to $1.63bn and pending | Charters can be reversed; arbitration yields money, not sovereignty |
| Liberland | 2015 to date | Danube floodplain, Croatia/Serbia | Unrecognised; Croatian police remove structures and fine occupants | Even a "terra nullius" claim on land fails |
| MS The World | 2002 to date | Global cruising, Bahamas flag | 165 privately owned residences under a conventional ship regime | Residential life at sea works as a ship, not a state |
| Freedom Ship | 1990s to date | Planned for Honduras (2000), now Indonesia | Never built; $15bn capital hurdle | Scale and capital kill projects before law does |

### 4.1 Sealand (1967 to date)

Roy Bates occupied Roughs Tower, an abandoned wartime anti-aircraft platform, and proclaimed the Principality of Sealand on 2 September 1967.[^24] In *R v Bates* (Essex Assizes, October 1968), Chapman J directed acquittal on firearms counts. Roughs Tower was "about three miles outside territorial waters", and the Firearms Act 1937 was not intended to have extraterritorial effect. The judge noted that "no one has suggested that Roughs Tower is a ship".[^25] The ruling concerned the reach of a statute. It did not recognise Sealand, and the Foreign Office's line was: "It's a fort in the North Sea, that's all. It's not a state ... it's a building."[^24] In 1978 the Administrative Court of Cologne dismissed a claim by a Sealand "Foreign Secretary" that his Sealand "citizenship" had cost him German nationality. It held that Sealand was not a state because an artificial platform is not territory and its members did not form a "people".[^11] The UK's Territorial Sea Act 1987 then extended the UK territorial sea to 12 nautical miles, which brought the tower inside it.[^26][^24] Sealand continues to exist because the UK finds it harmless, not because it holds any legal status.

### 4.2 Rose Island (1968 to 1969)

Engineer Giorgio Rosa built a 400 m² platform on piles off Rimini, just outside Italy's then territorial waters. He declared the "Esperanto Republic of Rose Island" on 1 May 1968, with stamps and plans for a currency. At 7 a.m. on 25 June 1968, 55 days later, Carabinieri and Guardia di Finanza officers took control. On 11 February 1969 the Italian Navy fixed explosives to the pylons and sank it.[^27] Italy acted without waiting for any international process. A sovereignty declaration close to a coast prompts immediate enforcement.

### 4.3 Republic of Minerva (1972)

Michael Oliver's Ocean Life Research Foundation dredged sand onto North Minerva Reef and declared independence on 19 January 1972. The plan was to earn revenue partly by registering ships under a flag of convenience.[^28] Tonga responded by building permanent above-water structures on the reefs. On 15 June 1972 King Taufa'ahau Tupou IV proclaimed that "the islands of Teleki Tokelau and Teleki Tonga and all islands, rocks, reefs, foreshores and waters lying within a radius of twelve miles thereof are part of our Kingdom".[^29] The South Pacific Forum recognised Tonga's "historical association" with the reefs and agreed "there could be no question of recognising other claims and specifically that of the Ocean Life Research Foundation".[^30] Any genuinely unclaimed feature will be claimed by a neighbour the moment someone tries to use it.

### 4.4 Operation Atlantis (1968 to 1970s)

Pharmaceutical entrepreneur Werner Stiefel planned an "independent shipboard nation", followed by a fixed island near the Americas. The ferro-cement ship *Atlantis II* capsized at its Hudson River launch, broke a propeller shaft off South Carolina, and was destroyed by a storm while anchored near Acklins Island in the Bahamas. A later attempt to build an artificial island at Silver Shoals ended when gunboats, reported as Haitian (one participant says Dominican), ordered the group to leave.[^31]

### 4.5 Seasteading Institute and Blue Frontiers, French Polynesia (2017 to 2018)

On 13 January 2017 French Polynesia signed a memorandum of understanding with the Seasteading Institute to "cooperate on creating legal framework" for a floating island with a "special governing framework" in an "innovative special economic zone".[^32] Blue Frontiers was formed to build it and later ran a token sale.[^33] The MoU was non-binding and expired at the end of 2017. In February 2018 the governing Tapura Huiraatira party stated that it "has no legal value" and "does not bind the Country in any way". This followed local opposition and a petition of about 1,700 signatures ahead of territorial elections.[^34][^35] The French government later told the National Assembly that the MoU had lapsed "without notable legal or financial progress".[^36] The lessons: an MoU is a letter of intent, not a right, and local politics in a small host can end a project within one electoral cycle.

### 4.6 Ocean Builders and the Thai seapod (2019)

In early 2019 Chad Elwartowski and Supranee Thepdet lived for about two months in a 6 m wide cabin on a spar anchored 14 nautical miles off Phuket, promoted as "the first seastead".[^37] The Royal Thai Navy seized and towed it in April 2019. Thai police charged the couple in absentia "with violating Thai sovereignty, punishable by the death penalty or life in prison", and revoked Elwartowski's visa. Thai authorities said the structure was inside Thailand's 200-mile EEZ. Ocean Builders said it was in international waters.[^38][^39] Under UNCLOS Thailand was entitled to regulate and remove an unauthorised structure in its EEZ (Art 60(1) and (2)). Calling it a breach of "sovereignty" stretched the concept, since the EEZ is a zone of sovereign rights rather than sovereignty. But the practical outcome shows what happens when a structure is placed in a coastal state's EEZ without its consent.

### 4.7 Ocean Builders in Panama (2020 to date)

Ocean Builders moved to Linton Bay Marina in Panama, where demonstration SeaPods sit about 1.2 km offshore.[^40] The company reports that its SeaPod gained "full legal approval as a houseboat vessel", allowing it to anchor outside the marina zone.[^41] Fixed or floating structures in Panamanian waters generally need a seabed concession or dock permit from the Panama Maritime Authority. Those concessions run for up to 20 years, renewable, with environmental permits, fees and inspections.[^42] This works precisely because it claims no autonomy at all.

### 4.8 Próspera ZEDE, Honduras (2020 to date)

Próspera is a land-based charter city, but it is the most important recent test of the "host-state charter" pathway. Honduras's 2013 constitutional amendment and ZEDE organic law allowed semi-autonomous zones with their own legal, administrative and tax systems. In 2021 Honduras Próspera signed a Legal Stability Agreement purporting to stabilise its regime until 15 January 2064, or ten years after any repeal.[^43] After a change of government, Congress repealed the ZEDE law in April 2022 (Decreto 33-2022). The constitutional repeal was not ratified in the following session, so the legal position was unclear for a time.[^44] On 20 September 2024 the Supreme Court declared the whole ZEDE framework unconstitutional with retroactive (*ex tunc*) effect.[^45][^43]

Próspera filed an ICSID claim under CAFTA-DR in December 2022 (ARB/23/2), initially for about $10.775 billion, roughly two-thirds of Honduran GDP. In October 2025 it reduced the claim to $1.63 billion, seeking restitution or damages in the alternative.[^46][^47] The tribunal has rejected Honduras's exhaustion-of-local-remedies objection and its request to bifurcate.[^43][^47] Honduras denounced the ICSID Convention in 2024, but President Asfura signed it again in March 2026. Próspera continues to operate, while the Honduran Attorney General says compliance with the court ruling "is not negotiable" even as conciliation is explored.[^48][^49]

The lesson cuts both ways. A treaty-backed stabilisation agreement gave investors real leverage, a live claim for compensation and continued de facto operation. It did not preserve the autonomous regime against a determined government and its own constitutional court. **Arbitration converts lost autonomy into a damages claim. It does not keep the autonomy.**

### 4.9 Liberland (2015 to date)

Vít Jedlička proclaimed Liberland in April 2015 on about 7 km² of Danube floodplain that neither Croatia nor Serbia claims in their border dispute. No state recognises it.[^50] Croatia administers the area, has detained visitors and removed structures, and in 2026 was still fining occupants for camping and enforcing mooring bans.[^50][^51] Even a land claim based on genuine *terra nullius* arguments has not produced recognition after a decade.

### 4.10 Residential ships: MS The World, Villa Vie and Life at Sea

MS *The World* (2002) is the working model of permanent residential life at sea. It is Bahamas-flagged, with 165 residences owned by about 150 families, 150 to 200 people aboard at a time, and about 300 crew. Residents vote on the itinerary three years ahead.[^52] It succeeds because it is unambiguously a passenger ship, fully compliant, insured, classed and calling at ports, and its residents keep their own nationalities and tax residences. The newer models are shakier. Life at Sea's three-year cruise was cancelled in November 2023 days before departure, and most of the more than 100 passengers were left chasing refunds.[^53] Villa Vie's *Odyssey* was stuck in Belfast from April to September 2024 with rudder and gearbox problems while it waited for final inspections.[^54]

### 4.11 Freedom Ship and MV Satoshi

These are often grouped together, but they were separate projects. *Satoshi* (Ocean Builders, 2020 to 2021) is covered in Section 3.3. Freedom Ship, a mile-long ship for tens of thousands of residents, was reported in 2000 as about to start construction in Honduras.[^55] In 2025 it remained a concept with an estimated $15 billion cost, and its chief executive named capitalisation as "the primary obstacle".[^56]

### 4.12 Host-state floating cities

Two state-backed projects sit within the recommended pathway. The **Maldives Floating City** is a public-private partnership between Dutch Docklands and the Maldives government in a lagoon near Malé. Its agreement was re-signed in 2022, and in late 2025 the architect reported that the first section had been shipped to the site.[^57] Delivery has been slow: in a right-to-information reply, the tourism ministry said no work had begun on the lagoons allotted to the developer and two of the five had been sold.[^58] **Oceanix Busan** was agreed in a 2021 MoU between Busan, UN-Habitat and Oceanix.[^59] It lost momentum after Busan's failed Expo 2030 bid, and in 2025 the city said it had still not heard how the $600 million would be financed.[^60] Both show that host consent is necessary but not sufficient: capital and political continuity decide delivery.

## 5. Personal legal realities for residents

### 5.1 Citizenship and passports

Residents will keep their existing nationalities. Any "seastead passport" would be a souvenir. The Sealand ruling shows how courts treat micronational citizenship: it is not a nationality and has no legal effect.[^11] Travel, consular protection and voting all stay tied to residents' home states, and so do the obligations.

### 5.2 US citizens: citizenship-based taxation, FATCA and expatriation

US citizens are taxed on worldwide income wherever they live. The relief available abroad, the foreign earned income exclusion (FEIE), requires a tax home in a "foreign country". The IRS defines this to include a country's territorial waters (within 12 nm) but "not ... international waters or airspace, nor ... offshore installations that are located outside the territorial waters of any foreign country".[^61] **A US resident living on a platform in a host's EEZ, or on the high seas, cannot claim the FEIE. The same person living on a platform in the host's territorial sea can.** This alone is a strong argument for pathway (a).

FATCA requires foreign financial institutions to report the accounts of US persons or face withholding, and requires US individuals to report foreign assets (Form 8938 and FBAR).[^62] Renouncing citizenship now costs a $450 consular fee, reduced from $2,350 with effect from 13 April 2026.[^63] Renunciation can trigger the expatriation tax for "covered expatriates", meaning those with net worth of $2 million or more, average annual net income tax above $206,000 (2025), or a failure to certify five years of compliance. Covered expatriates face a deemed sale of all assets, with an exclusion of $890,000 for 2025.[^64]

### 5.3 CRS and banking

Outside the US, the OECD Common Reporting Standard automatically shares account information with account holders' tax-residence jurisdictions. As of 30 September 2026, 131 jurisdictions had committed to exchanges.[^65] Banks must establish where each customer is tax resident. An unrecognised "seastead" residence will not satisfy a compliance department. In practice, and this is a judgement rather than a sourced finding, banks will either report residents to their previous country or close the account. A residence permit and address in a host state's SEZ, on the other hand, is ordinary and bankable.

### 5.4 Criminal jurisdiction and extradition

Criminal law follows residents through the **nationality principle**. The US claims jurisdiction over offences "by or against a national of the United States" in any place outside any nation's jurisdiction (18 USC 7(7)).[^9] Many other states extend serious offences to their nationals abroad. The judge in *R v Bates* gave murder, bigamy and treason by British subjects as examples.[^25] In a host zone, host criminal law applies territorially. Extradition treaties exist only between states, so an unrecognised seastead can neither request nor refuse extradition. A fugitive aboard is protected only until a navy decides to board under Article 110(1)(d). The realistic model, as with the UAE's financial free zones, is that criminal law stays with the host state while civil and commercial law is devolved (Section 7).

### 5.5 Family law, births and statelessness

Marriages, divorces, adoptions and custody orders need recognition by real states, so they must be made under host or home-state law. The Hague family-law conventions operate between states only. For children born aboard:

- The Convention on the Rights of the Child (Art 7) requires every child to be "registered immediately after birth" with "the right to acquire a nationality".[^66]
- The 1961 Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness (Art 3) deems a birth on a ship to have occurred in the territory of the flag state.[^67] UNHCR guidance confirms that this applies to registered vessels even in another state's waters, but "an essential condition in all cases is that the 'ship' is registered in a Contracting State".[^68]
- On an unflagged, unrecognised platform on the high seas, a child has no place of birth in any state. Nationality then depends entirely on descent from the parents. Some states restrict transmission of nationality to children born abroad, especially to parents who were themselves born abroad. Each family must check its own nationality law. Second-generation statelessness is a real risk.

In a host's territorial waters the birth is registered in the host state, and any jus soli or statelessness safeguards in host law apply. Practical protocol: residents give birth in the host state's hospital (the clinic is not a maternity unit; see the services chapter) and register with their own consulates.

## 6. Practical regulation

| Area | Requirement | Pathway (a): host territorial waters | Pathway (c)/(d): high seas |

| Classification | Class society rules for floating structures (DNV/ABS/BV) | Host can adopt class rules by reference in the zone statute | Required by flag state and insurers |
| Insurance | Property all-risks, liability, marine construction; P&I if vessel-like | Conventional, priceable | *Satoshi* precedent: no International Group cover |
| Ports and customs | Every supply run is an import or export | Customs-free zone within the SEZ; host port is a domestic run | Every run is an international voyage with full clearance at each end |

| Radio (VHF, GMDSS, AIS) | Ship-station or coast-station licence | Host coast-station licence | Flag-state ship licence; none if stateless |

| Search and rescue | UNCLOS Art 98 duty; host SAR region | Host coastguard | Nearest SAR state; long response times |

**Telecoms.** ITU Radio Regulations No. 18.1 provides that "no transmitting station may be established or operated by a private person or by any enterprise without a licence ... by or on behalf of the government of the country to which the station in question is subject".[^69] ITU Resolution 25 requires administrations that license global satellite systems to ensure the terminals can be operated only from territories whose governments have authorised them. Starlink's maritime service therefore offers "global coverage in international waters", but "network availability in territorial waters is subject to licensing by local authorities" and is geofenced where unlicensed.[^70] Before signing, the host must license satellite service for the zone.

**Aviation.** The UK CAA's CAP 437 (9th edition, amendment January 2026) sets the criteria for offshore helidecks on fixed and mobile installations and vessels.[^71] Medevac and charter operators will expect the deck to meet it or a host equivalent.

**Ports.** UNCTAD notes that free zones are "essentially separate customs territories".[^72] Making the seastead and a shore-side logistics plot a single customs-free zone removes most friction on supply runs.

### 7.2 Why (a) dominates (b)

The EEZ offers no extra legal autonomy, because Article 60(2) gives the host the same exclusive jurisdiction. It adds wave exposure, cost and logistics, removes the FEIE for Americans, and gives the host less day-to-day stake in the project. Its only advantage is distance from host politics and media, and that advantage disappears if anything goes wrong. A later phase could place an aquaculture or energy module in the EEZ under the same host agreement if there is a technical reason to do so.

### 7.3 Recommended structure for pathway (a)

The aim is to get as close to "self-government" as a sovereign host can lawfully grant, and to make each grant costly to reverse.

1. **Host selection.** A small, stable, maritime state, ideally one that already uses special zones or offshore registries, has a cyclone-free lagoon within the design basis latitude band, and has an investment treaty or free-trade agreement with the investors' home states. Criteria should include constitutional capacity to legislate special zones (avoid Honduras's problem of a zone that conflicts with the constitution), cross-party support, and local community acceptance. Judgement: shortlist on these criteria in the site-selection chapter.

2. **Host State Agreement (HSA).** A contract between the host government and the project foundation, **ratified by the legislature**, and where possible backed by an organic or constitutional provision. Core terms:
   - A **water-space and seabed concession of at least 75 to 99 years**. It must at least match the 50-year platform life plus decommissioning. Compare Panama's 20-year renewable maximum for seabed concessions.[^42]
   - A **zone statute** creating a customs-free, tax-light special zone. UNCTAD counts nearly 5,400 SEZs in 147 economies, so this is an ordinary tool.[^72] It should delegate to a zone authority the power to adopt building, safety and commercial rules (with class rules incorporated by reference), and to license businesses, telecoms (including satellite) and the helideck, within limits set by host and international law.
   - **Choice of civil and commercial law.** For example, English common law applied directly, as in Abu Dhabi Global Market,[^73] with zone courts or an arbitral tribunal for civil disputes. As in the UAE financial free zones, the host's **criminal law, defence, foreign affairs and immigration control remain with the host**.[^74] Negotiate a fast-track residence permit category for residents.
   - A **stabilisation clause** freezing the fiscal and regulatory deal for the concession term. The clause should carve out non-discriminatory health, safety, environmental and human-rights measures, following the UN Principles for Responsible Contracts.[^75] A narrower clause is more defensible and less politically toxic than a total freeze.[^76]
   - **Dispute resolution:** ICSID or UNCITRAL arbitration seated outside the host, with the investing entity structured through a state that has an investment treaty with the host, plus political-risk insurance.
   - **Exit and step-in:** a pre-agreed buy-out formula on termination, and the right to tow the platform away (the "exit option" that a floating structure uniquely offers) or sell it to the host at valuation.
   - **Local benefit:** host-national employment quotas, a revenue share or local equity stake, and public access to parts of the open space. Polynesia shows that the social licence is the real long-term stabiliser.

3. **Private-law governance inside the zone.**
   - A **foundation** (in the host zone or in a neutral common-law jurisdiction) owns the platform and holds the concession.
   - Residents hold **long leaseholds** of house plots (or shares in a cooperative) on terms that run with the concession.
   - A **residents' constitution**, operating as a covenant attached to every lease (homeowners' association style), sets out internal governance, budgets, rules and amendment procedures, with binding arbitration of disputes.
   - **Service contracts** for utilities, marine operations and security, which can be re-tendered.
   - **Membership and exit rules:** admission criteria, buy-back of leaseholds on departure, and expulsion only by arbitral decision.

This is "governance by contract" within a host's sovereign envelope. It is how condominium ships, gated communities, free zones and Próspera's own internal arrangements work.

## 8. What "libertarian sovereignty" looks like at sea in 2026

Honestly, it does not exist, and nothing in the current law, or in its foreseeable evolution, will make it exist for a group of 150 people on a man-made platform. Every legal route ends at an existing state: the host whose waters it occupies, the flag state whose ensign it flies, or the home states whose nationals live aboard and whose tax and criminal laws follow them. Distance from shore changes how quickly a state shows up, not whether it has the right to.

What can realistically be built:

- **Real self-government over daily life.** Who lives there, what gets built, how money is spent, how disputes are settled, which commercial rules apply, and much of what is taxed locally. This is more than most towns on land get, and comparable to the UAE financial free zones or the better charter-city models.
- **Real exit.** A floating structure that can be detached and towed is a stronger negotiating position than any land-based zone has. That threat, combined with an arbitration claim, is the community's main protection against bad faith from the host.
- **No passports, no seat at the UN, no immunity** from the host's criminal law or the residents' home-state taxes. Americans in particular remain fully American for tax purposes, and they lose the FEIE the moment the platform leaves the 12-mile line.

The precedents also show what goes wrong. Declaring sovereignty near a coast gets the project dismantled (Rose Island, Thailand). Choosing an "empty" place gets it annexed (Minerva). Choosing a host without locking in politics gets it disowned (Polynesia) or litigated (Próspera). Choosing ship status runs into SOLAS and P&I insurance (Satoshi). The projects that last either claim nothing (Ocean Builders in Panama, MS The World) or are too small and old to be worth removing (Sealand).

The honest pitch to the founding families should therefore be: "a self-governing floating village under a negotiated charter", not "a new country".

## 9. Design-basis assumptions this chapter challenges

1. **"Host-nation EEZ: more autonomy."** Incorrect in law. Under UNCLOS Art 60(2) the host's jurisdiction over an EEZ installation is exclusive, and in practice it differs little from the territorial sea. The EEZ also costs US residents the FEIE. *Proposed revision:* describe setting 2 as "more distance, the same legal autonomy, harsher sea".
2. **"High seas: maximum legal autonomy."** Incorrect. On the high seas a structure is either under a flag state's law or stateless, and statelessness means minimum legal protection. *Proposed revision:* "maximum distance, minimum legal protection; requires a flag state."
3. **"Floating polity / country."** Not achievable (Section 2). *Proposed revision:* "autonomous community under a host-state charter".
4. **The 50-year design life must be matched by a concession of at least the same term** plus decommissioning. Many hosts' standard concessions are far shorter (Panama: 20 years renewable). This is a precondition for financing.
5. **Visitors and residents counted as "passengers"** if the platform were ever treated as a ship would bring passenger-ship rules into force. The engineering chapters should design to classification rules for floating structures, not SOLAS, and the legal structure must keep it that way.
6. **Births aboard.** The clinic should not be planned as a maternity unit, and births should take place ashore in the host state.

## Verdict

> **Overall: AMBER.** A permanent, self-governing floating town is legally feasible only as an autonomous zone under a cooperating host state's sovereignty. As a sovereign country it is **RED**: a legal showstopper with consistent enforcement precedent.
>
> | Pathway | Rating | One-line reason |
> |---|---|---|
> | (a) Host special zone in territorial waters | **Amber** | Achievable and bankable; autonomy limited to what the host grants; political-reversal risk |
> | (b) EEZ installation with host consent | **Amber** (dominated by a) | No extra legal autonomy (Art 60(2)); harsher sea; US residents lose the FEIE |
> | (c) Flagged vessel on the high seas | **Red** (for this brief) | SOLAS passenger rules and P&I insurance make a 2-ha garden town impractical as a ship; flag state is sovereign |
> | (d) Declared sovereignty on the high seas | **Red** | Contrary to UNCLOS Arts 60(8), 89 and 121; stateless; boarding and prosecution precedents |
>
> **Top risks**
> 1. **Host political reversal** within the 50-year life (Próspera repealed after about 2 years of operation; Polynesia MoU disowned within 13 months). *Mitigation:* legislated HSA, constitutional backing, stabilisation clause, investment treaty access, local equity, exit/tow-away rights.
> 2. **Any sovereignty claim or unauthorised siting** triggers enforcement, up to criminal charges (Thailand 2019: death penalty or life imprisonment). *Mitigation:* make no sovereignty claims; site only with written host authorisation.
> 3. **Insurance and classification unavailability** (Satoshi 2020). *Mitigation:* classify as a moored floating installation; engage insurers and a class society before committing capital.
> 4. **Residents' home-state jurisdiction:** US worldwide taxation (no FEIE offshore beyond 12 nm), FATCA/CRS, and nationality-based criminal jurisdiction. *Mitigation:* site within territorial waters; give prospective residents individual tax advice; set realistic expectations.
> 5. **Child nationality and family-law gaps**, including statelessness risk for second-generation births. *Mitigation:* births and civil registration in the host state; residence-permit regime agreed in the HSA.
>
> Secondary risks: concession term shorter than design life; telecoms and helideck licensing; local community opposition; fraud and refund exposure if residences are pre-sold before the HSA is legislated (Life at Sea).
