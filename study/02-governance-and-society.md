# 02. Politics, governance and society

## Summary

Every serious attempt to found a libertarian polity in the last 55 years has failed for political or social reasons long before it failed for engineering ones. Minerva was annexed by Tonga within five months. Liberland's settlement was demolished by Croatian police. Ocean Builders' first seastead was towed away by the Thai navy, and its occupants were threatened with the death penalty. The French Polynesia deal was killed by a local election campaign. Próspera, the most successful project of the lot, has about 100 physical residents and is now fighting a sovereign state in international arbitration after that state repealed the law it was built on. The projects that last (Tristan da Cunha, Twin Oaks, the kibbutzim for most of the twentieth century) have tight membership boundaries, high trust, cheap ways of resolving conflict, a clear economic base and a legitimate relationship with an outside state.

For 100 to 150 people the governance problem itself is tractable. It is about the size of a large strata scheme, a ship's company or a village. We recommend a **hybrid**:

- a short **founding charter** that every adult signs as a contract;
- **strata-style property rights** in the hull and modules;
- an **all-resident assembly** plus a small **elected council**;
- a **professional, non-elected Master** whose emergency powers are narrow, triggered by defined events and time-limited;
- **mediation on board**, with binding **external arbitration** seated in Singapore (SIAC) or London (LCIA);
- **host-state criminal law and courts** for anything violent, sexual or involving children.

The hard problems are elsewhere: winning and keeping a host state, avoiding the "billionaire escape pod" story, banking access, children and teenagers, medical evacuation, founder dynamics and money running out. Our verdict is **Amber** for a community inside a host nation's sheltered waters and **Red** for any plan that relies on high-seas sovereignty.

## 1. Intellectual lineage: what was promised and what was delivered

### 1.1 Seasteading

The Seasteading Institute (TSI) was announced on 15 April 2008 to "establish permanent, autonomous ocean communities to enable experimentation and innovation with diverse social, political, and legal systems", building on Patri Friedman and Wayne Gramlich's work[^1][^3]. Peter Thiel pledged US$500,000 in seed money[^2].

The core claim is Friedman's **"dynamic geography"**: if buildings float, a household or neighbourhood can detach and leave a badly run jurisdiction, so "if the state tries to impose a sales tax on Monday, the capitol building may be all that's left of the city by Tuesday"[^4]. Friedman and Taylor's academic version concedes that "mobility is surely limited far more by work and social obligations than by the physical costs of relocation"[^5]. The ancestors are **Tiebout (1956)**, in which residents "vote with their feet" between competing jurisdictions, under assumptions of full mobility and many communities[^6], and **Hirschman (1970)**, who questions "the efficiency of the competitive mechanism, with its total reliance on exit" and gives *loyalty* the job of slowing exit so that *voice* can work[^7].

Seasteading is a bet on exit. A 150-person floating town will run on voice: a family whose house sits on a shared hull cannot sail away.

The political tone was set by Thiel's 2009 essay: "I no longer believe that freedom and democracy are compatible", with seasteading named as an escape from politics alongside cyberspace and space[^8]. That sentence has followed seasteading into every hostile press cutting since. Joe Quirk and Patri Friedman's 2017 book *Seasteading* (Free Press) took a greener, humanitarian line and presented French Polynesia as the first host[^9].

### 1.2 Charter cities and network states

Paul Romer's **charter cities** proposed new cities with new rules on empty land in developing countries, under some outside supervision. In Honduras, Romer chaired a Transparency Commission whose appointment decree was never published. He resigned in 2012 after the government signed a zone agreement he was not allowed to see[^10][^11]. Madagascar's earlier interest ended amid violent protest and the fall of President Ravalomanana[^12]. The lesson that runs through this chapter is that handing foreign groups control of territory is politically radioactive in post-colonial states.

Balaji Srinivasan's ***The Network State*** (2022) reverses the order, "cloud first, land last": an aligned online community with a "recognized founder" crowdfunds scattered physical nodes and seeks recognition at the end[^13]. It centres a founder (a governance risk, section 6) and treats territory as the last and hardest step.

### 1.3 Promised versus delivered

| Promise | Delivered (to Nov 2026) |
|---|---|
| Autonomous communities on the high seas | None. TSI moved to host-nation waters around 2013, then the French Polynesia MOU lapsed in 2018[^16] |
| "Vote with your house" competition between floating jurisdictions | No multi-module floating polity exists. Friedman himself now says seasteading has made little progress, that "the ocean is just very difficult and very expensive", and that land-based zones are "much more tractable"[^14] |
| New governance experiments | Mostly on land: Próspera (Honduras), plus network-state pop-ups |
| Funding from tech wealth | Seed grants, then a failed token sale. The Blue Frontiers *Varyon* presale missed its threshold during the 2018 crypto crash and buyers were refunded[^17]. Academic critics note that no major investor buy-in followed and that Thiel distanced himself[^15] |

The honest reading is that the idea has been an excellent generator of designs, conferences and media, and a poor generator of residents.

## 2. Case studies: what made them fail or succeed

### 2.2 Libertarian and new-polity projects

**Próspera** is the closest analogue to this project: a private charter, privately supplied rules and its own arbitration centre. Neighbouring Crawfish Rock objected early that few construction jobs went to villagers, that armed guards questioned residents, and that plans appeared to absorb village land[^18]. The promoter held four of nine council seats on seven-year terms[^19]. Honduras repealed the ZEDE law in 2022, and in 2024 its Supreme Court declared the framework unconstitutional from the outset[^20]. The investors' CAFTA-DR claim at ICSID is pending[^21]; Bloomberg put the original US$10.8 billion figure at roughly a third of Honduran GDP[^22], and it was later cut to US$1.63 billion[^20]. In 2026 Próspera reported 2,262 e-residents and about 100 physical residents[^23]. *Lessons:* a zone created by one government can be destroyed by the next; suing a poor country for a large share of its GDP may win in law but loses in politics; and low local benefit plus private security plus opaque land plans is a reliably bad combination.

**Liberland** (2015, 7 km² of Danube floodplain[^24]) has gathered huge numbers of online sign-ups, half a million by one BBC count[^26], but Croatian police control access and escorted demolition of its settlement in 2023[^25]. *Lesson:* online enthusiasm does not become physical presence, and whoever controls the access route controls the polity. For a seastead, that is the port, the fuel dock and the airport.

**The Free State Project** hit its 20,000-signature trigger in 2016, with about 2,000 already moved to New Hampshire[^27]. In Croydon, Free Staters used a town meeting attended by 34 voters to halve the school budget by 20 to 14; residents then turned out in historic numbers and reversed it by 377 to 2[^28]. In Grafton, a "Free Town" effort cut budgets and services, and the town became known for decay and bear attacks[^29]. *Lessons:* low-turnout direct democracy can be captured, capture produces fierce backlash, and even libertarians expect working schools, roads and wildlife control.

**Minerva** (1972): Michael Oliver's group raised a reef with Australian sand and declared a republic; Tonga proclaimed the reefs Tongan within months, sent troops, and the project collapsed amid infighting[^30][^31]. *Lesson:* neighbouring states act together against sovereignty claims near their waters.

**Blueseed** (2011–13) planned a ship 12 nautical miles off California for about 1,000 foreign entrepreneurs without US work visas[^32], and stalled for lack of funding[^33]. *Lesson:* even a sharp business case could not finance a vessel; a residential seastead's case is weaker.

**Ocean Builders** (2019) anchored a spar home 12 to 14 nautical miles off Phuket, and its occupants declared themselves independent of any state's law. Thailand said it was in its EEZ, charged them with violating sovereignty (potentially a capital offence) and towed the structure away as evidence[^34][^35]. The firm's Panama SeaPod prototype later tilted onto a dock at its 2022 launch in front of Panama's president[^36]. *Lesson:* never announce independence from a host whose navy is nearby.

### 2.3 Intentional communities

**Arcosanti** was designed for 5,000 but "has never been occupied by more than 100"[^37]; after the founder's death his daughter alleged abuse that his "inner circle knew about"[^38]. *Lesson:* a founder-centred community inherits both the vision and the shadow, and safeguarding cannot rely on the founder's circle.

**Twin Oaks** (1967, about 85 adults and 15 children) has outlasted almost every secular commune of its generation with mundane tools: a written labour-credit system, rotating planners with a right of appeal, a visitor period followed by an absence and a membership decision, and written procedures for conflict and for behaviour with "an intolerable impact"[^39]. *Lesson:* boring, written process beats charisma.

**Kibbutzim** handled free-riding, adverse selection and brain drain through screening, probation and communal property that made exit costly[^40]. After the 1985 debt crisis and the high-tech boom most moved to differential pay, and by 2010 only about a quarter stayed fully egalitarian[^41]. *Lesson:* founding idealism decays; institutions must work for a second generation that did not choose to be there.

Sosis found 19th-century religious communes outlived secular ones at every stage, which he attributes to costly commitment signals[^54]; Kanter links longevity to "commitment mechanisms"[^55]. A libertarian community has thin shared ritual by design, so it must lean harder on contracts, clear economic interest and good process.

### 2.4 Tiny remote communities

**Pitcairn** (UK territory, 47 people at the time) is the most important negative case. In 2004, seven men, about half the island's adult men, were tried on 55 sexual-offence charges reaching back decades; six were convicted. Defendants argued that underage sex was traditional and that British law did not apply, and some island women backed the tradition claim[^42]. By 2024 about 40 people remained, mostly elderly, and the school had closed because the last three children were at boarding school in New Zealand[^43]. *Lessons:* in a tiny closed polity the norm-enforcers may be the offenders, victims cannot leave or complain to anyone unrelated, and child protection needs an outside channel the community cannot capture.

**Tristan da Cunha** (about 230 people) is the positive counterpart. Land is communal, livestock is capped "to prevent better off families accumulating wealth", outsiders may not buy land or settle, and income comes from a regulated lobster fishery, stamps and tourism[^44]. Evacuated to England after the 1961 eruption, islanders voted 148 to 5 to return[^45]. Serious medical cases go to Cape Town, and it "can be up to 2 months before a ship is available"[^46]. *Lessons:* a clear economic base, shared identity, strict entry rules and a supportive outside state (a UK Administrator alongside an elected Island Council) produce durability, at the price of very low openness.

**St Kilda** was evacuated in 1930 at its own request. The petition says several of the men who "tend the sheep, do the weaving and look after the general welfare of the widows" were leaving and that the island "has not been self supporting"[^47]; decline accelerated as imports replaced self-sufficiency and wartime naval supply ended[^48]. *Lesson:* lose the working-age adults and the place fails within a generation.

### 2.5 Confinement and isolation research

**Biosphere 2**'s eight crew (1991–93) split into two hostile groups of four, and the split was made "much worse" by a power struggle among owners and advisers *outside* the dome[^49][^50]. **Antarctic research** describes a "winter-over syndrome" of poor sleep, impaired cognition, low mood and interpersonal conflict; about 5% meet psychiatric criteria, but many report growth, and screening and support help[^51]. A 2017 meta-analysis did not confirm the popular "third-quarter" slump[^52]. The 520-day **Mars-500** simulation recorded creeping inactivity and sleep–wake disruption in most crew[^53]. A sheltered-water seastead with a ferry to town is far less confining, but the lessons transfer: daylight, exercise, routine, privacy and outside contact protect mental health, and conflict among *external* stakeholders propagates inward.

## 3. Governance design for 100 to 150 people

### 3.1 Scale

Dunbar's 1992 extrapolation from primate brains gave a "natural" human group of about 150[^56]; a 2021 re-analysis found the method yields anything from about 4 to 520, so "specifying any one number is futile"[^57]. Use it loosely. At 100 to 150 people everyone knows everyone, gossip is an effective sanction and informal norms do much of the work, but recusal is nearly impossible. At Phase 2 (about 300 plus staff) informal control weakens, so write the charter for 300 from day one.

Ostrom's design principles for long-lived commons map almost exactly onto a shared hull: clear boundaries, locally fitted rules, collective choice, monitoring, graduated sanctions, cheap conflict resolution, recognition by the outer authority, and nested tiers[^58]. Seasteaders tend to ignore the seventh: outside authorities must not challenge the right to organise.

### 3.2 Options compared

| Model | Strengths | Weaknesses at this scale | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shareholder corporation / HOA (promoter-controlled) | Fast decisions, investor-friendly, familiar to banks | Residents become customers. Próspera-style promoter seats invite "company town" framing[^19] | Useful as the *asset* layer only |
| Direct-democracy town meeting | Legitimacy, cheap, suits about 100 adults | Low-turnout capture (Croydon)[^28], slow, bad in emergencies | Keep for charter and budget, with quorum rules |
| Constitutional republic (elected council, rights, courts) | Proven checks and balances, legible to host states | Heavy for 150 people. Courts are not viable locally | Lightweight version is the core |
| Private / polycentric law with arbitration | Matches libertarian ethos, enforceable worldwide via the New York Convention (172 parties)[^63] | Arbitration cannot handle crime, child protection or status matters. Cost can exclude poorer residents | Civil disputes only |
| DAO / on-chain governance | Transparent voting records | The DAO hack (2016) showed no capacity for emergency response[^59]. Across about 30,000 DAOs, participation is low and power concentrates as they grow[^60]. Regulators and banks are wary | Optional tool for recording votes, not the constitution |

### 3.3 Recommended hybrid

**Layer 1: legal wrapper (host-state).** A special zone or concession agreement with the host state (design-basis archetype 1). Criminal law, child protection, immigration, AML/CFT and maritime safety stay with the host. The community governs civil, property and municipal matters by contract. This is less romantic than sovereignty and far more survivable.

**Layer 2: asset layer (strata/condominium analogue).** A Platform Owners' Corporation owns the hull, moorings, utilities and common areas. Each household owns a lot (house module plus garden) with a *unit entitlement* that sets both levies and votes on asset questions. This mirrors strata law, for example in New South Wales, where owners automatically form the owners' corporation, levies are proportional to unit entitlement, and a 10-year capital works plan is required[^61]. NSW by-laws may not be "harsh, unconscionable or oppressive", may not ban children, and may not stop an owner from selling. Those are good minority-protection defaults to copy.

**Layer 3: civic layer (charter).**
- An **Assembly** of all adult residents. It votes on the charter, the annual budget and removals. Quorum is 60% of adults, which closes the Croydon loophole.
- A **Council** of five, elected by single transferable vote for staggered two-year terms, limited to three terms. It runs day-to-day policy and appoints officers.
- An **external Ombudsperson and Safeguarding Officer**, appointed jointly with the host authority or an independent NGO and paid from levies. They can take complaints from any resident, including children and staff.
- A **Master**, a professional marine officer who is hired rather than elected. The Master holds safety authority (section 3.6).

**Layer 4: disputes.**
1. A neighbour-mediation panel of trained residents.
2. Binding arbitration under **SIAC Rules 2025**. The Streamlined Procedure applies automatically to claims up to S$1 million: one arbitrator, normally on the documents, award within three months, capped fees[^64]. London (LCIA) is the obvious alternative for an Atlantic or European host.
3. Host-state courts for anything criminal, and for judicial review of the arbitration.

Próspera's choice of a captive in-house arbitration centre[^65] is cheaper but less credible. An external seat signals to banks, host states and residents that disputes will not be settled by the founders' friends.

### 3.4 Entry, exit and removal

- **Entry:** application, visit, 6 to 12 months' probationary residence (as Twin Oaks and the kibbutzim do[^39][^40]), then an Assembly vote. Criminal-record and child-safeguarding checks apply to every adult, staff included, along with host-state KYC for banking.
- **Exit:** an entrenched right to leave at any time. A lot may be sold to any buyer who passes entry vetting. The Corporation has a right of first refusal at an independently assessed value. A buy-back reserve funded from levies stops households being trapped by a thin resale market. This is the floating version of Hirschman's insight: exit has to be real, but not so easy that the community bleeds.
- **Removal of a bad actor:** graduated sanctions, in the order warning, fine, suspension of amenities, then removal[^58]. Removal requires a written finding of serious or persistent breach, a hearing, a two-thirds Assembly vote, and a right to arbitral review. The person's property is then sold at fair value; it is never confiscated. New York's courts have upheld exactly this model for cooperatives that terminate an "objectionable" shareholder's lease under a pre-agreed contractual clause, provided the board acts in good faith and within its authority[^62]. Anything that may be a crime goes to the host police, not to the Assembly.

### 3.5 Funding public goods

There is no income tax. Revenue comes from:

1. **strata levies** by unit entitlement, split into an operating fund and a **capital works / sinking fund** covering hull maintenance over the 50-year design life;
2. **metered user charges** for power, water and berths;
3. **commercial rents** from the café, guest rooms and marina;
4. a **visitor fee**;
5. an **emergency and evacuation reserve** held off-platform with a regulated custodian.

Levy arrears accrue interest and ultimately trigger forced sale, as in strata schemes. Operating costs belong to the economics chapter, but staff (section 8) will be the largest recurring item.

### 3.6 Emergency powers: a ship needs a captain

International maritime law already resolves the tension. SOLAS regulation V/34-1 says no owner, charterer "or any other person" may prevent the master from taking any decision that "in the master's professional judgement, is necessary for safety of life at sea and protection of the marine environment"[^66]. We recommend copying both the principle and its limits:

- **Scope:** safety of life, the hull's integrity, pollution prevention and evacuation. *Not* property, speech, membership, budgets or the charter.
- **Trigger:** objective events (a named storm warning, hull breach, fire, medical mass-casualty) or a Council resolution.
- **Duration:** 72 hours, renewable by Council for 72 hours at a time, with automatic expiry. This follows the Venice Commission's tests of necessity, proportionality and temporariness, and its view that emergencies must not change the constitution[^67].
- **Accountability:** a written log, and an after-action review to the Assembly within 30 days. The Master can be dismissed by the Council for cause, but not *during* the emergency.

Libertarians can accept this because it is contractual and known in advance, exactly as passengers accept a master's authority when they board a ship.

### 3.7 Amendment and minority protection

- Ordinary charter amendments need a two-thirds Assembly vote *and* 75% of lots, with a 90-day cooling-off period.
- **Entrenched articles** cannot be amended while anyone who relied on them is resident. These are the right of exit, non-confiscation, children's rights, non-discrimination, the external ombudsperson and arbitration.
- **Founder sunset:** any special founder or investor seats lapse automatically at the earlier of five years or 60% occupancy. Próspera's seven-year, unlimited-term promoter seats[^19] are what to avoid.

### 3.8 Children's rights and education

Children are about a third of the launch population and did not consent to the charter. It should guarantee (a) education to recognised qualifications (host-state or international curriculum) with external inspection; (b) host-state child-protection law, the external safeguarding officer and mandatory reporting by every adult; and (c) a funded right to off-platform secondary or tertiary study and an unconditional right to leave at 18 with an exit grant. Pitcairn shows what happens without (b)[^42][^43].

## 4. Social sustainability

### 4.1 Demographics and the teenage problem

Tristan educates children to 16, then they board in the UK or South Africa if they want more[^69]. Pitcairn's children usually leave at 13, and many do not return[^70]. About 35 children aged 0 to 17 means two or three teenagers finishing each year, each one a family deciding whether to follow. Our judgement: plan for **families leaving when their eldest reaches 14 to 16** and recruit to offset it. Practical responses are a site within a daily ferry ride of a good secondary school (archetype 1); three to four teachers; blended online secondary courses (contested, as the Croydon micro-school plan showed[^28]); and a deliberate age mix, because the second generation decides survival[^41][^48].

### 4.2 Healthcare

A town of 100 to 150 people with 20 visitors is comparable to a small cruise ship. The cruise industry's ACEP guidelines, which CLIA members follow for ships carrying 100 or more people, require at least one physician *and* one other clinician available 24 hours a day at sea. They also call for paediatric and trauma life-support training, an emergency and stabilisation room, an intensive care bed and an isolation room[^68]. Covering that around the clock needs **three to four clinicians on rotation**. That is more than a third of the design basis's staff allowance on its own.

The decisive variable is evacuation time:

| Setting | Indicative time to hospital (judgement) | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Archetype 1, sheltered host waters, port and airport nearby | 30 min to 2 h by boat or helicopter | GP-plus clinic is acceptable |
| Archetype 2, 12 to 200 nm offshore | 2 to 8 h, weather-dependent | Need a stabilisation capability plus a helideck |
| Archetype 3, high seas | Days. Tristan waits up to two months for a ship[^46] | Not acceptable for families with infants, pregnancies or elderly members |

Births should be planned ashore. Dialysis, chemotherapy and elective surgery mean temporary relocation.

### 4.3 Isolation, "island fever" and mental health

"Island fever" is a folk term, but the underlying risks (poor sleep, inactivity, low mood, conflict) are well documented in isolated groups[^51][^53]. Mitigations from polar programmes: screening and a pre-move trial stay, confidential off-platform counselling, private space in every home, funded shore leave (at least one trip a month per resident), structured roles and routine, and rotation for staff. Some residents will simply leave; that is the system working.

### 4.4 Conflict resolution

Disputes over noise, children, dogs, boats, money and romance will dominate, and there is nowhere to cool off. Twin Oaks-style written procedures, a trained mediation rota and a clear escalation path[^39] are worth more than any constitutional clause. Biosphere 2 adds: keep investor and founder fights out of daily life and give residents honest information[^49].

### 4.5 Gender, family and ageing

Libertarian projects skew male; the Grafton newcomers were "overwhelmingly men"[^29], and Twin Oaks keeps a dedicated letter for prospective women members[^39]. For families the risk is the **trailing partner**, isolated with small children while the enthusiast thrives. Require both adults to complete the probationary stay, and design childcare and work space for the less enthusiastic partner. Domestic violence needs a host-police pathway and an off-platform refuge agreement from day one. Ageing founders will need step-free access, home care and eventually a move ashore; recruit retirees with eyes open.

### 4.6 When founders disagree or money runs out

Minerva's founder sacked its provisional president as the project collapsed[^31]; Biosphere 2's owners fought while the crew were sealed inside[^49]. Plan for both:

- **Founder disputes:** founders hold no governance role beyond ordinary votes after the sunset date. Investor rights sit in the asset layer, not the civic layer. Disputes among investors go to arbitration, not to the Assembly.
- **Insolvency:** the sinking fund and evacuation reserve are ring-fenced. Lenders and the host get step-in rights to keep life-safety systems running. A pre-agreed **decommissioning plan** covers towing, sale or scrapping, so that "money ran out" means an orderly sale rather than a St Kilda-style petition[^47].

## 5. Geopolitics and reputation

### 5.1 How host states and great powers will see it

To a host state, a self-governing enclave in its waters is a sovereignty question first and an investment second. Thailand's response to two people in a cabin shows the reflex[^34]. Great powers will mostly ignore a 5-acre village. They will care if it touches sanctions (ship-to-ship transfers, opaque ownership, "flag hopping"), crypto flows, or their own citizens' tax[^79]. The EU's tax blacklist, revised twice a year, currently names eight jurisdictions, including several small Pacific and Caribbean islands[^78]. A host on, or near, that list makes banking and EU relations harder.

### 5.2 Case: French Polynesia, 2017 to 2018

The agreement was announced at Christmas 2016 and signed in January 2017 as a non-binding memorandum. Local voices immediately worried that "rich Americans" wanted "to dodge taxes", and critics called it "tech colonialism"[^71]. Once the Atimaono lagoon at Mataiea was named as the site, about 80 residents formed the association *No to'u Here ia Mataiea*, complaining of a lack of information, and opposition candidates made it an issue in the 2018 territorial election campaign[^72]. The government then declared the memorandum void, "not a legal document" that had expired at the end of 2017[^73][^74]. TSI blames a "small minority" spreading "misinformation" plus a crypto crash[^16]. *Lessons:* the site was named before local consultation; the deal rested on a non-binding paper signed by one administration in an election year; the visible benefits (tax-free zone, technology) went to outsiders; fishing grounds and a culturally significant lagoon were at stake; and financing depended on a token sale that failed[^17]. Every one of these is avoidable.

### 5.3 The "billionaire escape pod" frame

Douglas Rushkoff's widely read 2022 account of super-rich "preppers" puts seasteading in the same breath as bunkers in New Zealand and plans for Mars[^75]. Academic critics describe seasteads as "the promise to investors one of great luxury", with "no actual prospects for building affordable housing"[^15]. This chapter's judgement is that the frame cannot be argued away, only out-competed with facts. Useful facts would include local equity, local jobs, open public space, published accounts, a scientific programme and residents who are visibly not billionaires.

### 5.4 Banking and FATF

The FATF says banks should manage risk case by case rather than "wholesale cutting loose of entire classes of customer"[^76]. In practice, de-risking is real. The Pacific islands have lost correspondent-banking relationships for two decades, which raises costs and pushes flows into informal channels[^77]. A new micro-jurisdiction associated with crypto and tax avoidance is a textbook de-risking candidate.

Mitigations: bank through a regulated host-state institution with full KYC; publish the Corporation's beneficial ownership; adopt a written AML/CFT policy to host and FATF standards; issue no community token; appoint a compliance officer; and make clear that residents remain responsible for their home-country tax, so the community is visibly *not* a tax shelter.

### 5.5 How to win a host state over

| Offer | Why it works |
|---|---|
| Local equity stake or golden share for the host or municipality | Turns a "foreign enclave" into a joint venture. The Maldives Floating City is a government joint venture aimed at housing locals[^80][^81] |
| Local hiring and procurement targets, published annually | Answers the Crawfish Rock complaint about jobs[^18] |
| Pay host taxes on commercial activity and a concession fee | Removes the "tax haven" story |
| Public access to the park, marina and café | Converts the platform from a gated compound into a destination |
| Climate-adaptation demonstrator with a university partner, with open data | Gives ministers a reason to defend it. Note that Oceanix Busan stalled for lack of US$600 million in financing, so bring the money first[^82] |
| Consultation *before* site selection, with fishers, landowners and customary authorities | The French Polynesia failure in one line |
| Binding legal instrument (statute-backed concession) with cross-party support, not an MOU | An MOU died with one election[^73]; even Próspera's constitutional basis proved reversible[^20], so political breadth matters as much as legal form |

## 6. Who would actually move?

| Segment | Likely share (judgement) | Needs | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Remote professionals and founders (tech, finance, creative) | 40–50% | Fast, redundant internet, co-working, travel links | Footloose. Leave when the novelty fades |
| Early retirees and "semi-retirees" | 15–25% | Healthcare, accessibility | Medical evacuation burden |
| Entrepreneurs running on-platform businesses (hospitality, marina, aquaculture, tours) | 10–15% | Customers, permits | Small market |
| Marine scientists and conservation staff (institutional partner) | 5–10% | Lab space, grants | Turnover with grant cycles |
| Crypto-wealthy idealists | 5–15% | Ideological fit | Concentration of wealth and influence, reputational risk |
| Resident staff (marine ops, clinic, school) | 15–25 people | Pay, rights, housing | Two-class society if staff lack a vote |

Remote work is now mainstream. MBO Partners counts 20.3 million US workers self-describing as digital nomads in 2026[^83]. The pool of people who *could* live on a seastead is therefore large. The pool who will commit a family and a house to it is small. Patri Friedman himself warns that pop-up gatherings "select for the rootless", people who "are generally not the ones who will settle permanently, raise families, and build the long-term fabric of a community"[^14]. Recruit for staying power, not enthusiasm.

**Cults of personality.** The network-state model places a "recognized founder" at the centre[^13]. Arcosanti and Pitcairn show what happens when no one can challenge the people at the centre[^38][^42]. Warning signs: one person controlling admissions, money *and* communications; critics recast as disloyal; pressure to sell land-based homes; secrecy about finances. The founder sunset, external ombudsperson, external arbitration and published accounts are the antidote.

## 7. Draft outline of a founding charter

1. **Purpose and nature.** A voluntary community formed by contract. It operates under the [host] Concession Agreement and does not claim sovereignty.
2. **Relationship with the host state.** Host criminal, immigration, child-protection, AML/CFT and maritime-safety law apply. Liaison procedures.
3. **Rights of residents (entrenched).** Life, bodily integrity, speech, association, privacy, property, due process, non-discrimination, and the right to leave at any time.
4. **Children (entrenched).** Recognised education, safeguarding with mandatory reporting, an external safeguarding officer, and the right to leave at 18 with an exit grant.
5. **Membership.** Admission, probation and vetting. Categories: owner, tenant, staff, visitor. Political rights of staff after 12 months.
6. **Property.** Lots and unit entitlements, common property, rules on alteration, sale and right of first refusal, and non-confiscation (entrenched).
7. **The Assembly.** All adults. Quorum 60%. Powers over the charter, budget, removals and by-laws.
8. **The Council.** Five members, STV, staggered two-year terms, three-term limit. Executive and appointment powers.
9. **Officers.** Master, Treasurer, Clinician-in-charge, Head Teacher, Compliance Officer. How they are appointed and dismissed.
10. **Emergency powers.** The Master's safety authority. Triggers, 72-hour limit, renewal, log and review.
11. **Finance.** Levies, user charges, sinking fund, evacuation reserve, external audit and published accounts.
12. **Conduct and sanctions.** By-laws, graduated sanctions, removal procedure, and forced sale at fair value.
13. **Disputes.** Mediation, then SIAC (or LCIA) arbitration, then host courts for crimes and review.
14. **Ombudsperson.** An independent external appointment with powers to investigate and publish.
15. **Founders and investors.** Rights confined to the asset layer. All special seats sunset at five years or 60% occupancy.
16. **Amendment.** Two-thirds of the Assembly plus 75% of lots, a 90-day cooling-off, and entrenched articles listed.
17. **Dissolution and decommissioning.** Ring-fenced reserves, step-in rights, and a plan for the hull.

## 8. Comments on the design basis

1. **"Country" and sovereignty.** For politics and governance, only archetype 1 (host-nation sheltered waters under a binding concession) is viable for a family community. Archetypes 2 and 3 should be treated as later experiments, not launch options. Every case that asserted independence from a nearby state (Minerva, Liberland, Ocean Builders) was ended by that state.
2. **Resident staff of 10 to 15 is too low.** A 24/7 clinic needs three to four clinicians[^68], the school three to four teachers, round-the-clock marine watch and engineering five to eight, plus administration and compliance. **Plan for 18 to 25.** The charter must also decide whether staff vote. A disenfranchised service class is both a moral and a reputational problem.
3. **About 35 children at launch is the hardest possible demographic** (section 4.1). Consider launching with a mix weighted towards couples without children and families with under-11s, plus a secondary-education plan agreed before anyone moves.
4. **Phase 2 (about 300 plus 30 staff) is well beyond village scale.** The charter and staffing should be designed for it from the start.
5. **Communal space.** The 1,500 m² for the school, clinic, town hall, café, workshop and guest rooms is tight once the clinic meets cruise-style guidelines. Add room for a counselling and quiet space and for an emergency refuge.

---

> ### Verdict: **AMBER** (feasible with significant caveats)
>
> **Feasible:** governing 100 to 150 people on a shared hull, using strata-style property, a contractual charter, an elected council, a narrowly empowered Master and external arbitration. Every component has working precedents.
>
> **Not feasible (Red):** any plan that depends on recognised sovereignty or on high-seas autonomy for families with children.
>
> **Top risks**
> 1. **Host-state reversal.** An election or court ruling removes the legal basis (Próspera, French Polynesia). *Mitigate:* a binding concession with cross-party support, local equity, local jobs and host taxes.
> 2. **Reputational framing** ("billionaire escape pod", tax haven, crypto), which drives banking de-risking and political hostility. *Mitigate:* transparency, no token, regulated banking, public access and a research partner.
> 3. **Demographic attrition.** Teenagers and their families leave, and working-age adults are lost (St Kilda, Pitcairn). *Mitigate:* a site near schools, an age mix and active recruitment.
> 4. **Safeguarding failure in a closed community** (Pitcairn, Arcosanti). *Mitigate:* host law, external safeguarding officer and ombudsperson, and vetting of all adults.
> 5. **Founder and investor conflict, or insolvency,** spilling into daily life (Biosphere 2, Minerva). *Mitigate:* a founder sunset, separating the asset layer from the civic layer, ring-fenced reserves and a decommissioning plan.
